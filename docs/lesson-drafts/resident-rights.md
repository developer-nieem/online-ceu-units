## Module: Rights Foundation (`rights-foundation`)

### Lesson: Federal Resident Rights Overview (`federal-rights`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Explain the source of federal resident rights in long-term care (OBRA).
- List major resident rights CNAs must uphold during daily care.
- Recognize when a rights violation requires reporting.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Where Resident Rights Come From
Federal law sets minimum standards for nursing homes that participate in Medicare or Medicaid. The Nursing Home Reform Act (part of OBRA, 1987) requires facilities to protect each resident's rights and dignity. California adds stronger state rules, but federal rights are the nationwide foundation.

As a CNA, you are on the front line of these rights every day—in bathing, dining, toileting, and conversation. Violations are not only "big events." Small daily disrespect—ignoring preferences, exposing bodies in hallways, gossiping about residents—erodes rights and can trigger survey citations and harm.

##### Core Federal Rights You Touch Daily
**Right to dignity and respect.** Treat each person as an adult. Knock before entering. Cover during care. Use preferred name and pronouns when known.

**Right to be fully informed** about care and changes in plain language. You do not give medical diagnoses, but you can explain what you are doing during care.

**Right to participate in care planning** to the extent possible. Ask about preferences: bath time, music, male/female caregiver if available and policy allows.

**Right to refuse treatment** (with limits). Residents may refuse a shower today. They cannot demand unsafe acts. Notify nurse when refusals affect health.

**Right to privacy and confidentiality.** Close doors and curtains. Do not discuss residents in public areas. Protect health information under HIPAA facility policies.

**Right to be free from abuse, neglect, and exploitation.** Zero tolerance. Report suspected abuse immediately per California mandatory reporting rules.

**Right to voice grievances** without retaliation. Support residents who wish to speak to ombudsman or administration. Never punish complaints.

**Right to manage personal funds** and possessions with facility safeguards.

##### Special Topics: Restraints and DNR
Physical restraints (including some restrictive devices) require physician orders and strict federal criteria—they are not for staff convenience. CNAs follow care plans and report improper restraint use.

Do Not Resuscitate (DNR) and POLST forms are medical orders. Follow facility policy for verification before emergencies. Do not assume DNR status—check orders and identifiers per protocol.

##### Advocacy Is Part of Your Role
Advocacy means speaking up for fair treatment. If a coworker rushes care disrespectfully, redirect kindly or notify supervisor. If a resident seems afraid of a visitor, report concerns. Ombudsman programs in California provide free resident advocacy—know how to share contact information per facility policy.

#### KEY POINTS
- OBRA federal law establishes minimum nursing home resident rights.
- Dignity, privacy, informed participation, and refusal rights apply during routine CNA care.
- Abuse, neglect, and retaliation for complaints are prohibited.
- Restraints and DNR require proper orders—never assume.
- CNAs advocate by respectful care and prompt reporting of violations.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** A resident refuses evening hygiene. Colleague says, "Just do it anyway—she won't remember." What is correct?

**Answer:** Respect informed refusal when safe. Explain benefits, offer alternatives, notify nurse if refusal risks skin issues or infection, and document. Forcing care violates dignity and may violate rights unless incapacity and legal processes apply per team direction.

**Question 2:** You overhear staff laughing about a resident's incontinence in the dining room. What should you do?

**Answer:** Stop the conversation if safe, remind staff about privacy and dignity rights, notify supervisor if ongoing, and support a respectful culture. This behavior violates confidentiality and federal dignity standards.

### Lesson: California-Specific Requirements (`california-requirements`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Describe California additions to federal resident rights in long-term care.
- Explain CNA responsibilities under California Title 22 concepts (scope-level).
- Identify resources for resident advocacy available in California.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### California Builds on Federal Standards
California Department of Public Health (CDPH) licenses skilled nursing and intermediate care facilities. State regulations (Title 22) and the California Advocates for Nursing Home Reform (CANHR) educational materials emphasize strong resident protections. Facilities must post rights, provide copies in language residents understand, and train staff—including CNAs—on rights.

California emphasizes:
- **Personal and civil rights** in state licensure rules
- **Mandatory abuse reporting** for all mandated reporters, including CNAs
- **Elder abuse prevention** training requirements tied to CNA certification renewal
- **Privacy** in multi-bed rooms (screens, scheduling, knocking)

When federal and state rules differ, the more protective rule for the resident generally applies.

##### Rights in Daily California Practice
**Language access.** Offer interpreters for important discussions. Do not rely on other residents to translate private health information.

**LGBTQ+ residents** deserve respect for identity, clothing, and chosen family. Use correct names. Report discriminatory treatment.

**Cultural and religious practices** such as diet, prayer times, or modesty during care should be accommodated when safe and feasible. Notify charge nurse to update care plan when preferences are ongoing.

**Visitation and access** follow federal and state updates; know current facility policy on visiting hours and resident choice to receive visitors.

##### Grievance Process and Ombudsman
Residents may file grievances verbally or in writing. Facilities must respond within set timeframes under federal requirements. CNAs should know how to help a resident reach social services or the administrator without blocking the process.

California Long-Term Care Ombudsman Program provides free, confidential advocacy. Phone numbers are posted in facilities. If a resident asks for help complaining, connect them to ombudsman or facility grievance coordinator per policy.

##### Documentation Supports Rights
Accurate, respectful documentation proves care respected choices. Example: "Resident requested door closed during care—honored. Preferred female caregiver when available."

Report rights concerns through supervisor and mandated reporting channels when abuse or neglect is suspected.

#### KEY POINTS
- California Title 22 and CDPH rules add to federal resident protections.
- Language, cultural, religious, and identity respect are rights issues.
- Know grievance paths and California Ombudsman resources.
- Help residents access complaints without fear of retaliation.
- Document preferences and refusals objectively to show rights-based care.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** A Spanish-speaking resident nods during a care conference but later tells you they did not understand. What should happen?

**Answer:** Notify nurse or social services to reschedule with qualified interpreter. Informed participation requires understanding—not just presence. Document need for language services.

**Question 2:** Family demands access to another resident's room to visit their spouse, but roommate objects to open curtain during personal care. How do you balance rights?

**Answer:** Prioritize dignity and privacy of the roommate—close curtain, reschedule care, enforce roommate privacy rights. Arrange spouse visit in shared space when both agree. Escalate ongoing conflicts to nurse or social services.

## Module: CNA Advocacy (`advocacy`)

### Lesson: Privacy and Dignity (`privacy-and-dignity`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Apply privacy practices during personal care and in shared rooms.
- Protect health information under HIPAA at the CNA level.
- Support resident modesty, choice, and autonomy in daily tasks.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Privacy Is More Than a Closed Door
Privacy includes body, space, belongings, and information. In shared rooms, extra steps are required:
- Pull curtain or screen before care; ask roommate to step out briefly if possible
- Keep voices low when discussing care at bedside
- Cover resident during transfers in hallways; use blankets or robes
- Knock and wait before entering any room

Residents have the right to private communication with family, clergy, ombudsman, and attorneys. Do not linger unnecessarily during visits.

##### Dignity During Personal Care
Personal care is vulnerable. Strategies that preserve dignity:
- Explain each step before touch
- Offer choices: wash face first vs. feet, music on/off
- Use warm towels, avoid overexposure time
- Address residents by preferred title unless they invite first-name use
- Provide same-gender caregiver when requested and available

For residents with dementia, dignity still matters. Slow pace, eye contact, and calm tone reduce shame during incontinence care.

##### HIPAA for CNAs
HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) limits who can receive health information. You may share information with:
- Team members involved in care
- Family/friends the resident has authorized or who are present with resident permission

You may not:
- Discuss residents on social media
- Share information in elevators with unrelated visitors
- Leave charts or lists visible in public areas

Report suspected HIPAA breaches to privacy officer or supervisor.

##### Autonomy and Supported Decision-Making
Many residents retain decision-making ability even with memory problems. Offer simple choices: "Red shirt or blue shirt?" Too many choices overwhelm. Respect "no" when safe. When capacity is unclear, nurse and interdisciplinary team lead—CNAs report observations.

#### KEY POINTS
- Privacy covers body, space, belongings, and health information.
- Curtains, covering, low voices, and knock-first protect shared-room dignity.
- HIPAA restricts casual sharing; social media posting about residents is prohibited.
- Offer real choices during care; respect refusals and report health impacts.
- Dementia does not remove the need for respectful, modest care.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** A visitor asks you in the hallway how another resident is doing after surgery. What is your response?

**Answer:** Politely decline to share protected health information: "I can't discuss other residents' health. You can ask the nurse or the resident directly if appropriate." Redirect to privacy policy.

**Question 2:** During incontinence care, a resident covers their face and cries. What dignity-focused actions help?

**Answer:** Slow down, reassure, maximize covering, close curtain, explain steps, offer pause, use calm voice, complete efficiently without rough handling, and notify nurse if emotional distress is new or severe. Document behavior and interventions.

### Lesson: Rights Knowledge Check (`quiz`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Review major federal and California resident rights concepts.
- Apply rights principles to realistic long-term care scenarios.
- Prepare for course assessment on advocacy and compliance.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Rights Review for Practice
This lesson consolidates rights knowledge for clinical application and exam readiness. Remember the CNA's daily impact:
- **Dignity and respect** are active behaviors, not slogans.
- **Informed consent and refusal** require communication and nurse involvement when risk exists.
- **Privacy and HIPAA** protect information and modesty.
- **Freedom from abuse and restraints** is absolute without proper legal and medical processes.
- **Grievances and ombudsman access** cannot be blocked by staff inconvenience.

California CNAs are **mandated reporters** of abuse. Rights protection and abuse reporting overlap—when dignity is violated severely, reporting is both a right and a legal duty.

##### Scenario Patterns to Recognize
**Scenario type: Forced care.** Forcing food, hygiene, or medications against an alert resident's refusal may violate rights unless specific capacity and legal processes apply. Always notify nurse.

**Scenario type: Privacy breach.** Discussing resident bowel habits in public areas or posting photos violates dignity and HIPAA.

**Scenario type: Retaliation.** Extra rough care or ignoring call lights after complaints is illegal retaliation. Report to supervisor or ombudsman pathway.

**Scenario type: Improper restraint.** Tying wheelchair trays for wandering without orders, or using bed sheets as restraints, is high-risk. Only follow authorized care plans.

##### Documentation as Advocacy
Objective documentation protects residents and staff:
- What resident preferred or refused
- Who was notified and when
- How privacy was maintained
- Witnessed rights concerns

Avoid judgment words ("stubborn," "attention-seeking").

##### Preparing for Final Assessment
Review OBRA rights categories, California emphasis on abuse reporting, HIPAA basics, restraint rules, DNR/POLST handling at CNA scope, and ombudsman role. Exam questions often test **best action** when rights conflict with staff convenience—choose resident-centered, nurse-involved, policy-following options.

#### KEY POINTS
- Daily CNA actions either uphold or undermine federally protected rights.
- Forced care, privacy breaches, retaliation, and improper restraints are high-priority violations.
- Mandated abuse reporting complements rights advocacy.
- Objective documentation demonstrates compliance and protects residents.
- Exam answers should prioritize dignity, safety, nurse notification, and policy.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** A resident wants to go outside in a wheelchair but the weather is cold and windy. Nurse approved outdoor time with blanket. Colleague says it's too much trouble. Best action?

**Answer:** Support the resident's right to participate in activities when nurse-approved and safe. Assist with blanket, time limit, and monitoring. Report if staffing blocks authorized activities routinely—a systemic rights issue.

**Question 2:** You discover a signed POLST indicating DNR, but wrist identifier is missing before lunch. What do you do?

**Answer:** Notify nurse immediately to verify orders and correct identifiers per facility emergency policy. Do not assume status without verification. Ensure team communication before potential emergency.
