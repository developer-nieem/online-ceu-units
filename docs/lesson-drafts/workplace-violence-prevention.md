## Module: Course Content (`main`)

### Lesson: Recognition & De-escalation (`recognition`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Recognize warning signs of workplace violence in healthcare settings.
- Apply de-escalation techniques safe for CNAs.
- Explain California SB 553 employer requirements at staff awareness level.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### What Counts as Workplace Violence
Workplace violence is any act or threat of physical violence, harassment, intimidation, or disruptive behavior that affects employees at work. In healthcare, sources include:
- **Residents or patients** with cognitive impairment, pain, or psychiatric crisis
- **Visitors and family members**
- **Coworkers** (bullying, sexual harassment)
- **Intruders** or criminals entering facilities
- **Domestic violence** spilling into workplace

California CNAs face high injury rates from assaults compared to many industries. Violence is not "part of the job." Facilities must prevent, respond, and support staff.

##### Warning Signs Before Escalation
Early signs may be nonverbal:
- Clenched fists, jaw, pacing, redness in face
- Loud or rapid speech, profanity
- Throwing objects, slamming doors
- Sudden withdrawal then explosive anger
- Paranoia or accusations
- Intoxication of visitor

Late signs: raised weapons, swinging arms, spitting, biting, choking. Exit and call for help before late stage when possible.

Environmental triggers include crowding, long wait times, loud alarms, poor lighting, and staff speaking harshly. Reduce triggers when safe: lower noise, offer privacy, assign consistent caregivers.

##### De-escalation Techniques for CNAs
De-escalation lowers emotional temperature without arguing facts:
1. **Ensure safety:** identify exits, call coworker or security per policy, remove other residents from area
2. **Calm voice and slow speech:** lower your volume; anger invites anger
3. **Increase distance:** stand at angle, not directly in face; respect personal space unless immediate harm
4. **Listen and validate feelings:** "I see you are upset about the wait."
5. **Set clear limits:** "I want to help, but I cannot let you hit staff."
6. **Offer choices within bounds:** "We can talk here or step to a quiet room with the nurse."
7. **Avoid threats and shame:** do not say "calm down" repeatedly or mock

Never turn your back on actively aggressive person if stay is required for resident safety—follow team plan.

##### Physical Holds and Restraints
CNAs generally do **not** perform physical takedowns unless specific trained program (some facilities train limited defensive techniques). Default: retreat, summon help, protect head and neck if attacked, use environmental barriers. Restraining residents requires physician orders and federal/state criteria—not for staff convenience.

##### California SB 553 Overview
Senate Bill 553 (effective phases 2024–2025) requires most California employers, including healthcare employers, to establish **Workplace Violence Prevention Plans (WVPP)**. Staff should know:
- How to access the written plan
- How to report incidents and near-misses
- Training frequency and participation records
- Hazard identification for your unit (psychiatric residents, dementia unit, night shift, entry points)
- Post-incident response and investigation process
- Prohibition against retaliation for reporting

Employers must maintain violent incident logs and Cal/OSHA reporting for serious events. CNAs contribute by reporting accurately.

##### Reporting and Documentation
After any incident or threat:
- Ensure medical care for injured persons
- Notify supervisor and security per policy
- Preserve evidence (camera retention, witness names)
- Complete incident report same shift with objective facts
- Seek workers' comp for work-related injuries

Near-misses (almost hit, verbal threat) should also be reported—they predict future violence.

##### Environmental Prevention
- Adequate staffing and functional alarms
- Panic buttons or phones accessible
- Lighting in parking and hallways
- Visitor management and sign-in
- Securing weapons policy enforcement
- Training on dementia behaviors and trauma-informed care

Support post-incident debrief and EAP counseling—violence causes psychological injury too.

#### KEY POINTS
- Violence comes from residents, visitors, coworkers, and intruders—zero tolerance.
- Watch early behavioral cues; intervene with calm voice, distance, validation, and limits.
- CNAs avoid untrained physical confrontation; call team and follow WVPP.
- California SB 553 requires employer WVPP, training, logging, and no retaliation.
- Report incidents and near-misses; document objectively same shift.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** Visitor shouts and kicks chair toward you but has not hit anyone. Best actions?

**Answer:** Keep distance, call supervisor/security per WVPP, clear area, use calm limit-setting ("I will help when it is safe to talk"), do not turn back, document threat and notify management for incident log.

**Question 2:** Resident with dementia slaps you during care. De-escalation steps?

**Answer:** Stop task if unsafe, protect yourself without excessive force, ensure resident and others safe, notify nurse, assess pain/UTI triggers, document objectively, follow care plan behavior interventions—not punishment. Report as workplace violence incident per policy.

### Lesson: Final Exam (`final-exam`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Integrate violence recognition, de-escalation, SB 553, and reporting concepts.
- Choose safe responses in healthcare violence scenarios.
- Prepare for final course assessment.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Comprehensive Review for CNAs
Healthcare workplace violence prevention combines:
- **Awareness** of who can perpetrate violence and early warning signs
- **De-escalation** communication and environmental calming
- **Personal safety**—retreat, team response, no untrained takedowns
- **Employer responsibilities** under California SB 553 WVPP
- **Reporting** incidents, near-misses, and Cal/OSHA serious events
- **Environmental controls**—lighting, staffing, access, alarms
- **Post-incident support**—medical care, workers' comp, counseling

Violence prevention is ongoing, not one training. Annual training and unit-specific hazard review are part of SB 553 compliance.

##### High-Risk Settings and Times
Higher risk may occur:
- Evening and night shifts with fewer staff
- Dementia units during personal care
- Admission and discharge stress
- Psychiatric or detox holds in hospital settings
- Waiting areas with angry family
- Parking structures

Plan exits before entering volatile rooms when history known. Read care plan behavior sections and precaution flags.

##### Sexual Harassment as Workplace Violence
Unwanted comments, touching, or requests from residents, visitors, or staff are reportable. You may request assignment changes after report per policy. Document patterns; one-time firm boundary may suffice for confused resident but repeated sexual behavior needs care plan update.

##### Weapons and Active Threat
If weapon present, do not approach. Secure residents if safe, evacuate, call 911 and facility security, follow active threat training (run-hide-fight as last resort per employer training). California facilities increasingly drill these scenarios.

##### Exam Question Patterns
Look for:
- **Best first action** when tension rises (distance, help, calm voice)
- **Mandatory reporting** of threats even without injury
- **SB 553** elements (plan access, no retaliation)
- **Avoid** fighting unless imminent deadly harm and trained
- **Document** facts, not opinions

Passing final exam (typically 80%) demonstrates readiness for certificate. Review recognition lesson examples and California employer obligations.

##### Culture of Safety
Teams that blame victims ("you must have provoked them") hide patterns. Speak up in safety huddles about recurring violent residents or visitor issues. Management must implement engineering and staffing controls, not only "be careful" messages.

Your safety enables resident safety—you cannot help others if injured or traumatized.

#### KEY POINTS
- Violence prevention spans recognition, de-escalation, environment, SB 553 plans, and reporting.
- High-risk times/settings need planning and staffing awareness.
- Harassment and weapons scenarios have specific escalation to security/911.
- Exam favors de-escalation, team help, documentation, and policy compliance.
- Safety culture requires reporting near-misses without blame.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** Coworker tells you not to file incident report after resident bite "because it will make the unit look bad." Correct action?

**Answer:** File report anyway—SB 553 and resident safety require accurate incident logging; retaliation is prohibited. Seek medical care and workers' comp if injured.

**Question 2:** During final exam scenario, confused resident grabs your hair during combing. Best answer choice likely includes?

**Answer:** Stop combing, protect head safely, call for assistance, notify nurse, document behavior and triggers, follow care plan/de-escalation—not slapping resident or ignoring injury risk.
