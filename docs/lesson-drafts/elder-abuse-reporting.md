## Module: Recognition (`recognition`)

### Lesson: Types of Abuse and Neglect (`types-of-abuse`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Define physical, emotional, sexual, financial, and neglect forms of elder abuse.
- Distinguish intentional abuse from neglect and self-neglect concepts.
- Identify institutional abuse patterns relevant to CNAs in facilities.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Who Is Protected in California
California law protects **elders** (generally age 65 and older) and **dependent adults** (ages 18–64 with physical or mental limitations restricting ability to carry out normal activities or protect rights). Most nursing home residents meet these definitions. CNAs are **mandated reporters**—you must report suspected abuse to authorities, not just your supervisor.

Reporting is required when you have **reasonable suspicion** based on observations or credible information. You do not need proof. Waiting for proof can endanger residents and expose you to legal risk for failing to report.

##### Physical Abuse
Physical abuse includes hitting, slapping, pinching, rough handling, inappropriate restraint, overuse of medications as chemical restraint, or force-feeding. Signs may include bruises, welts, burns, fractures, bite marks, or repeated "unexplained" injuries. Patterns matter: injuries in various healing stages, injuries inconsistent with explanation, or fear of a specific person.

CNAs must never use corporal punishment. Rough transfers, dragging, or pinching during care are reportable if intentional or reckless.

##### Emotional or Psychological Abuse
Verbal assaults, threats, humiliation, isolation, or terrorizing are emotional abuse. Examples: mocking incontinence, threatening nursing home placement to control behavior, or deliberately ignoring call lights as punishment. Residents may become withdrawn, anxious, or suddenly aggressive. Emotional abuse often occurs without visible marks—your documentation of witnessed language and behavior is critical.

##### Sexual Abuse
Any non-consensual sexual contact or exposure, including when dementia affects capacity, is abuse. Signs: genital bruising, torn underwear, sexually transmitted infections, fear during care, or resident statements. Preserve privacy and report immediately to nurse and authorities per policy.

##### Financial Abuse
Misuse of resident funds, coercion to change wills, stealing belongings, or staff using resident money for personal purchases. CNAs may notice missing jewelry, unauthorized purchases, or a resident afraid to discuss money with family.

##### Neglect
Neglect is failure to provide goods or services necessary to avoid physical harm or pain. It can be by caregivers or self-neglect in community settings. In facilities, neglect includes failure to provide food, fluids, hygiene, repositioning, medications (nursing responsibility but CNA must report missed care), shelter, or safety. Untreated bedsores, dehydration, filthy clothing, or unchanged wet briefs may indicate neglect.

##### Abandonment and Abduction
Deserting a dependent person or removing them without authority is reportable. CNAs should follow exit and visitor policies to prevent unauthorized departures.

#### KEY POINTS
- California protects elders (65+) and dependent adults; CNAs are mandated reporters.
- Report on reasonable suspicion—proof is not required before reporting.
- Abuse types include physical, emotional, sexual, financial, neglect, and abandonment.
- Institutional neglect includes missed basic care and unsafe environments.
- Document objective observations; patterns and fear behaviors are significant.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** You see a coworker twist a resident's wrist to force a spoon into their mouth. The resident cries out. What type of abuse is this, and what is your duty?

**Answer:** This is physical abuse (rough handling/force). Intervene if safe to stop immediate harm, ensure resident safety, notify nurse/supervisor, and make mandated report to Adult Protective Services (APS) and/or law enforcement per California timelines. Document facts.

**Question 2:** A resident with adequate care plan has unchanged saturated briefs for hours and developing severe diaper dermatitis. Is this abuse?

**Answer:** It may be neglect—failure to provide necessary hygiene and skin care. Report to nurse immediately for treatment and investigate staffing or supervision failures. Mandated reporting may be required if pattern indicates neglect.

### Lesson: Resident Risk Factors (`risk-factors`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- List resident characteristics that increase vulnerability to abuse and neglect.
- Describe perpetrator risk patterns CNAs may observe in facilities and homes.
- Apply prevention awareness during daily care and observation.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Why Some Residents Are More Vulnerable
Abuse is never the victim's fault. However, certain factors increase risk:
- **Cognitive impairment or dementia**—difficulty reporting, memory gaps dismissed by others
- **Physical dependency** for ADLs, mobility, toileting
- **Communication barriers**—aphasia, hearing loss, language differences
- **Social isolation**—few visitors or advocates
- **History of family violence** or substance abuse in relationships
- **Mental health conditions** including depression
- **Financial assets** attracting financial exploitation

Residents without regular visitor involvement need extra staff advocacy. Watch for "invisible" residents during busy shifts.

##### Perpetrator Patterns
Perpetrators may be family, visitors, other residents, or staff. Red flags include:
- Staff who avoid assigned residents, overmedicate behavior, or humiliate residents
- Family members who intimidate residents or insist on being alone with them against policy
- Residents who bully or sexually act toward roommates—report through facility process

Stress and burnout do not excuse abuse, but short staffing and poor training increase risk environments. Speak up about unsafe systems.

##### Environmental and Cultural Factors
Crowded rooms, lack of privacy, and poor supervision increase opportunity for abuse. Cultural norms about elder respect vary; abuse can still occur within families appearing "involved." Do not ignore signs because a family seems devoted publicly.

##### Prevention at CNA Level
- Know residents individually—baseline mood and appearance
- Rotate high-risk rooms with team awareness
- Encourage meaningful activities and social connection
- Report staffing patterns that make neglect likely before harm occurs
- Support peer accountability—zero tolerance for abusive jokes or rough care

California facilities must have abuse prevention policies and staff training. Your observations feed quality improvement and APS investigations.

#### KEY POINTS
- Dementia, dependency, isolation, and communication barriers increase vulnerability.
- Perpetrators may be staff, family, visitors, or other residents.
- High-stress environments raise neglect risk—report unsafe staffing early.
- Baseline knowledge of each resident helps detect new injuries or fear.
- Prevention includes advocacy, supervision, and team accountability.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** A resident with advanced dementia has frequent unexplained bruising on upper arms. Family says "she bumps into furniture." What should you consider?

**Answer:** Consider possible abuse or rough handling during transfers. Bruising on arms may suggest gripping injuries. Document pattern, compare to mobility, notify nurse, and initiate mandated reporting and internal investigation if suspicion remains.

**Question 2:** New resident has no visitors and staff nickname them "the easy one" for minimal care. What risk does this pose?

**Answer:** Social isolation plus minimized care is neglect risk. Ensure full care plan delivery, advocate for social work referral, monitor skin and hydration closely, and report if basic needs are skipped.

## Module: Reporting (`reporting`)

### Lesson: Mandatory Reporting in California (`mandatory-reporting`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- State California mandated reporter obligations for CNAs.
- Identify when and how to report to Adult Protective Services and law enforcement.
- Explain timelines and protections for reporters.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Legal Duty to Report
California Welfare and Institutions Code requires mandated reporters—including CNAs, nurses, physicians, and facility administrators—to report known or suspected abuse of elders and dependent adults. Report when you observe, know, or reasonably suspect abuse in your professional capacity or within the facility.

**Two reporting pathways often apply:**
1. **Internal immediate notification**—supervisor, administrator, nurse per facility policy
2. **External mandated report**—County **Adult Protective Services (APS)** and sometimes **local law enforcement**

For incidents in long-term care facilities, also follow **Long-Term Care Ombudsman** notification requirements when applicable. Facilities cannot require you to investigate before reporting externally.

##### Timelines Matter
California law requires reports **as soon as practicable** when abuse is observed, known, or suspected. For telephone reports, follow up in writing within two working days on SOC 341 form when required. Telephone immediately when danger is imminent.

If resident is in immediate danger, call 911 first, then APS. Document times of all calls.

##### What to Report
Provide resident identifying information, nature of suspected abuse, dates, locations, witnesses, and your contact information. Facts only—avoid diagnosing intent. Example: "0300 3/10: Resident stated son hit hand with belt last night; 2 cm bruise dorsal right hand; photo per policy; nurse notified 0305."

##### Reporter Protections and Confidentiality
Reports are confidential to protect reporters and victims. California law provides immunity from civil or criminal liability for mandated reports made in good faith. Employers **cannot legally retaliate** against you for mandated reporting. If retaliation occurs, document and seek legal or union support.

You may also report anonymously to APS in some cases, but identified reports help investigations. Follow facility policy while meeting legal minimums—when conflict exists, legal duty to report externally prevails.

##### APS Role
APS investigates community and facility-related abuse reports, coordinates with law enforcement, and arranges protective services. CNAs may be interviewed. Be honest, factual, and cooperative.

##### Special Situations
- **Resident-to-resident abuse:** Report and implement supervision plans.
- **Death with suspicion:** Report and preserve evidence per policy.
- **Domestic violence visitors:** Safety plan and law enforcement involvement.

#### KEY POINTS
- CNAs must report suspected elder/dependent adult abuse externally—not only internally.
- Report as soon as practicable; call 911 for immediate danger.
- SOC 341 written follow-up may be required after phone report.
- Good-faith reporters have legal immunity; retaliation is prohibited.
- Provide factual observations; APS investigates further.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** You suspect neglect but management says "handle it internally first." What is correct?

**Answer:** California mandated reporters must report suspected abuse/neglect to APS (and law enforcement if appropriate) without delay. Internal notification does not replace external reporting. Document your report.

**Question 2:** A resident asks you not to tell anyone their daughter took their pension money. What do you do?

**Answer:** Explain you are required to report suspected financial abuse to protect them. Notify nurse/supervisor and APS per timeline. Financial exploitation of elders is reportable even if resident fears family conflict.

### Lesson: Documentation Essentials (`documentation`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Document suspected abuse observations objectively and promptly.
- Avoid documentation errors that harm investigations or court cases.
- Coordinate documentation with mandated reporting steps.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Documentation Supports Justice and Safety
Good documentation creates a clear record for investigators, protects residents from ongoing harm, and supports staff who report in good faith. Poor documentation—vague, judgmental, or delayed—allows abusers to continue and weakens cases.

Write **immediately after** observation or disclosure when possible. Late entries must be labeled per facility policy with reason for delay.

##### What to Include
- **Date and time** of observation or resident statement (use quotes for exact words when possible)
- **Location** and who was present
- **Objective description** of injuries: size, color, location, resident report of pain
- **Behavioral signs:** fear, flinching, withdrawal
- **Actions taken:** nurse notified (name/time), supervisor, APS called (time, case number if available)
- **Care provided:** wound care, comfort, safety measures

Example: "1400: While assisting with sleeve change, noted 3 cm yellow-green bruise on L upper arm. Resident states, 'My roommate hit me with call button last night.' Resident tearful, requested room door closed. Charge RN Smith notified 1405. Incident report completed. Resident remained in room; roommate separated per RN."

##### What to Avoid
- Opinions: "definitely abused"—state facts and suspicion reported
- Labels: "liar," "dramatic"
- Diagnoses: "fractured hip" unless confirmed—use "complains of hip pain, leg shortened externally rotated"
- Blaming victim: "resident fell again because noncompliant"

Do not photograph injuries unless facility policy and consent/privacy rules allow—follow protocol.

##### Chain of Custody Awareness
Preserve physical evidence when directed: unchanged clothing, linens, or objects involved in alleged sexual assault. Do not bathe resident before medical forensic exam if sexual abuse suspected—follow nurse and law enforcement guidance.

##### Coordination With SOC 341 and Incident Reports
Facility incident reports are internal. SOC 341 (or current mandated form) goes to APS. Information should be consistent but not copy-paste vague phrases. Each document serves different legal purposes.

##### Self-Care After Disturbing Events
Documenting abuse is stressful. Use EAP, debrief with nurse, and follow violence prevention policies if reporting leads to threats.

#### KEY POINTS
- Document immediately with times, quotes, objective injury descriptions, and notifications.
- Avoid opinions, victim-blaming, and unconfirmed medical diagnoses in notes.
- Follow evidence preservation protocols for serious allegations especially sexual abuse.
- Internal incident reports complement but do not replace APS reporting.
- Accurate records protect residents and mandated reporters.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** Which note is best? A) "Abused by staff." B) "0300: 4 cm linear red mark across buttocks noted during bath; resident cried, said 'he hit me;' CNA Jose present; RN notified 0302."

**Answer:** B is objective, timed, descriptive, identifies notifications—appropriate for legal and clinical use. A is vague and conclusory.

**Question 2:** Resident discloses sexual abuse during toileting. What documentation and action sequence is correct?

**Answer:** Ensure immediate safety, do not bathe away evidence per guidance, notify nurse and law enforcement/APS per policy, obtain medical exam as ordered, document resident's words in quotes and objective findings, complete incident report, and provide emotional support within scope.

## Module: Course Assessment (`assessment`)

### Lesson: Reporting Knowledge Check (`reporting-quiz`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Integrate recognition, risk, reporting, and documentation concepts.
- Apply California mandated reporting rules to case scenarios.
- Prepare for final course assessment on elder abuse.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Integrated Review
Elder abuse prevention for CNAs rests on four pillars:
1. **Know types**—physical, emotional, sexual, financial, neglect, abandonment
2. **Know vulnerable residents**—dementia, isolation, dependency
3. **Report fast**—APS and law enforcement when indicated; do not delay for internal approval only
4. **Document facts**—times, observations, quotes, actions

California renewal requires elder abuse education. Your certificate documents training; your daily choices protect lives.

##### Decision Algorithm for CNAs
When you see or hear something concerning:
- **Step 1:** Ensure immediate safety (separate parties, call 911 if danger now)
- **Step 2:** Notify charge nurse/supervisor per policy
- **Step 3:** Provide urgent care within scope (comfort, skin protection)
- **Step 4:** Report externally to APS/law enforcement as mandated
- **Step 5:** Document objectively and complete required forms
- **Step 6:** Cooperate with investigation; no retaliation against reporters or victims

##### Common Exam Traps
- Choosing to "investigate first" before APS call—incorrect for mandated reporters
- Assuming family can refuse report on financial abuse—report still required
- Thinking only physicians report—CNAs are mandated
- Documenting conclusions without observations—weak and noncompliant

##### Facility vs. Community
CNAs may work in SNFs, assisted living, or home care. Reporting duty follows the victim's status as elder or dependent adult, not setting. APS handles community and facility reports; ombudsman complements resident advocacy in licensed facilities.

##### Cultural Humility in Reporting
Fear of deportation or family shame may silence victims. Reassure that reporting seeks safety, not punishment of victims. Use interpreters. Follow facility trauma-informed approaches while meeting legal duty.

#### KEY POINTS
- Safety first, then nurse notification, external APS report, documentation—in that priority.
- CNAs are mandated reporters with immunity for good-faith reports.
- Exam answers favor immediate reporting over internal-only handling.
- Documentation must be factual and timely.
- Training plus action prevents ongoing harm.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** You reasonably suspect a visitor is financially exploiting a resident but have no proof. Correct action?

**Answer:** Report suspicion to supervisor and APS as soon as practicable. Mandated reporting does not require proof. Document observations (missing funds, resident statements, unusual withdrawals if known).

**Question 2:** Coworker threatens you if you "snitch" after you witnessed rough handling. What do you do?

**Answer:** Prioritize resident and personal safety, report abuse to APS and law enforcement as required, notify administration, document threats, follow workplace violence policy, and seek whistleblower protections. Retaliation is illegal.
