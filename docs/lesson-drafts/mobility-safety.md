## Module: Body Mechanics (`body-mechanics`)

### Lesson: CNA Ergonomics Fundamentals (`ergonomics`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Describe safe body mechanics that protect your back and joints during care.
- Identify equipment and environmental factors that reduce injury risk.
- Apply California OSHA and facility policies related to safe patient handling.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Why Body Mechanics Matter
CNAs perform thousands of lifts, turns, and transfers each year. Poor technique causes back, shoulder, and knee injuries—the most common reason caregivers leave the field. Safe body mechanics protect you and the resident. A sudden lift without planning can cause falls, skin tears, or fractures for frail older adults.

Body mechanics means using your body in balanced, efficient ways. The goal is to let large leg and core muscles do the work—not your spine.

##### Core Principles
**Plan before you move.** Read the care plan and transfer orders. Know weight-bearing status, assist level, and equipment needed. Clear the path. Lock wheelchair brakes. Raise bed to working height. Remove obstacles and loose rugs.

**Base of support.** Stand with feet shoulder-width apart. One foot slightly forward for balance. Keep knees bent. Avoid twisting your spine—move your feet to turn.

**Keep loads close.** Whether lifting a limb or assisting a stand pivot, keep the resident close to your center of gravity. Hugging a gait belt is closer and safer than pulling at arm's length.

**Use legs, not back.** Bend hips and knees. Keep back straight. Lift smoothly without jerking. Exhale on exertion.

**Team lifts when required.** If the care plan says assist of two or mechanical lift, do not attempt alone. California facilities increasingly follow safe patient handling programs. Ask for help early.

##### Equipment That Supports Ergonomics
- **Gait belt** on waist over clothing (not on skin, ribs, or abdomen if contraindicated per plan)
- **Slide sheets** for repositioning in bed
- **Mechanical lifts** (Hoyer-type) for non-weight-bearing residents
- **Transfer boards** when ordered
- **Raised toilet seats and grab bars** to reduce deep squatting

Inspect equipment before use. Report frayed slings, broken brakes, or missing batteries immediately.

##### When to Stop and Reassess
Stop if the resident becomes dizzy, combative, or fatigued. Lower them safely and notify the nurse. Never drag a resident by arms or shoulders. Do not exceed your training on mechanical lifts—only staff trained and authorized per facility policy may operate them.

#### KEY POINTS
- Plan moves, widen base, bend knees, and avoid spinal twisting.
- Keep residents close; use legs for power, not your back.
- Follow care plan assist levels; use mechanical lifts when ordered.
- Inspect and use equipment correctly every shift.
- Stop unsafe moves and get help before injury occurs.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** You need to move a heavy resident up in bed alone because the unit is busy. Is this acceptable?

**Answer:** No. Follow the care plan assist level. If assist of two or a mechanical lift is required, wait for help or use ordered equipment. Working alone against the plan risks injury to you and the resident.

**Question 2:** While pivoting a resident to a chair, you feel sharp pain in your lower back. What should you do?

**Answer:** Safely lower or seat the resident with help if needed, secure their safety, stop lifting duties, report injury to supervisor, complete incident/workers' comp process per policy, and seek medical evaluation. Do not continue lifting through pain.

### Lesson: Transfer Techniques (`transfer-techniques`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Perform stand-pivot and seated transfer steps in correct sequence.
- Use a gait belt safely during ambulation and transfers.
- Explain when bed, chair, toilet, and mechanical lift transfers differ.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Before Any Transfer
Explain steps to the resident even if they have dementia—calm voice reduces resistance. Gather equipment: gait belt, walker, wheelchair with locked brakes, non-skid footwear. Ensure the resident is alert enough to participate or that mechanical lift is ordered for limited participation.

Position equipment close. Bed lowest safe height, flat or slight sit position as ordered. Dangle legs before standing if orthostatic hypotension is a risk—watch for dizziness.

##### Stand-Pivot Transfer (Bed to Chair)
1. Move chair at 45-degree angle to bed, brakes locked.
2. Apply gait belt snugly over clothing at waist.
3. Help resident to sit edge of bed, feet flat, hands pushing on bed if able.
4. Stand facing resident; bend knees; grasp gait belt at sides or use palm-up hold per facility training.
5. On count, resident stands with even weight bearing as tolerated.
6. Pivot resident's back toward chair; guide hips to seat.
7. Remove belt only after resident is stable and positioned.

Never pull upward under the resident's arms. This can dislocate shoulders or injure wrists.

##### Seated Transfer (Wheelchair to Toilet)
Align wheelchair with toilet at slight angle. Lock brakes, raise footrests or swing them aside. Apply gait belt. Resident scoots forward, stands briefly, pivots, and lowers to toilet with your guided control. Maintain dignity with clothing management and door privacy.

For residents who cannot bear weight, use mechanical lift with nurse-approved sling type and attachment points.

##### Mechanical Lift Overview (Conceptual for CNAs)
Only trained staff operate lifts. Steps generally include: select correct sling, attach per manufacturer, check hooks/clip, lift slowly, clear path, lower gently into chair. Never leave a resident suspended. Two staff may be required. Report unusual resident discomfort during lift—skin tears and fear can occur if rushed.

##### Documentation and Reporting
Note assist level used, resident tolerance, equipment, and any near-fall. Changes in ability to transfer may signal infection, stroke, medication effect, or pain—report promptly.

#### KEY POINTS
- Prepare equipment, footwear, and environment before every transfer.
- Use gait belt at waist; guide with legs bent; pivot—do not yank arms.
- Match technique to weight-bearing status and care plan.
- Mechanical lifts require training, correct sling, and never leaving resident hanging.
- Document tolerance and report declining transfer ability.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** A resident becomes lightheaded when dangling at bedside. What is the safest action?

**Answer:** Lower them back to a supported position, stay with them, obtain vitals if within scope and policy, notify nurse, and do not force standing until assessed. May need gradual progression or lift per updated orders.

**Question 2:** During a pivot transfer, the gait belt rides up to the chest. What should you do?

**Answer:** Stop, lower resident safely if needed, reposition belt over hips/waist per training, and restart. A high belt can injure ribs and gives poor control.

## Module: Fall Prevention (`fall-prevention`)

### Lesson: Fall Risk Factors (`risk-factors`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- List intrinsic and environmental factors that increase fall risk in long-term care.
- Interpret common fall risk tools used in California facilities (conceptual).
- Describe CNA observations that should be reported to reduce fall risk.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Falls Are Preventable Events—Not Accidents
Falls are a leading cause of injury and death among older adults in nursing homes. Many falls are preventable with assessment, equipment, and consistent routines. As a CNA, you are the eyes and ears on the floor. You see rushed hallway clutter, wet floors, and residents trying to stand alone.

##### Intrinsic Risk Factors (Inside the Person)
- **Mobility problems:** weakness, Parkinson's, stroke, foot pain
- **Cognitive impairment:** forgetting limitations, wandering, poor judgment
- **Sensory loss:** vision, hearing, neuropathy in feet
- **Medical issues:** low blood pressure when standing, abnormal heart rhythm, infection, dehydration
- **Medications:** sedatives, blood pressure drugs, diuretics increasing bathroom urgency
- **Incontinence:** rushing to bathroom unassisted
- **Prior falls:** fear or unsafe compensations

Any sudden change—new confusion, fever, refusal to walk—can spike fall risk. Investigate and report.

##### Environmental Risk Factors
- Poor lighting, especially at night
- Wet or waxed floors, loose cords, clutter
- Beds left too high; wheels unlocked
- Missing or nonworking bed/chair alarms when ordered
- Incorrect footwear (slippers without backs)
- Furniture placement blocking safe paths

Correct what you can immediately (wipe spill, lock brake). Report maintenance issues through work orders.

##### Facility Risk Assessment Tools
Many California facilities use standardized tools (such as Morse Fall Scale or similar) scored by nursing on admission and after changes. CNAs may not score tools but must follow resulting interventions: fall mats, alarms, toileting schedules, hip protectors, close observation, or assist levels.

Read the care plan every shift. Interventions only work when applied consistently.

##### Your Role in Observation
Report:
- New unsteadiness or grabbing furniture
- Resident attempting unsafe independent transfers
- Dizziness, new weakness, or infection signs
- Noncompliance with glasses, hearing aids, or walker
- Fear of falling limiting mobility (needs PT referral)

Timely toileting and hydration reduce urgent rushing—a common fall trigger.

#### KEY POINTS
- Falls have personal, medical, medication, and environmental causes.
- Sudden changes in status require nurse assessment and plan updates.
- Follow care plan fall interventions every shift without shortcuts.
- Fix immediate hazards; report equipment and maintenance needs.
- Toileting schedules and proper footwear are high-impact prevention steps.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** A previously independent resident tries to climb out of bed at night confused and agitated. What factors increase fall risk here?

**Answer:** Cognitive change (possible delirium), unsupervised transfer attempt, likely poor lighting, and possible infection or pain trigger. Stay with resident, call nurse, ensure alarm and low bed per plan, and do not leave until safe plan is in place.

**Question 2:** You find a resident's walker stored across the room while they walk to the bathroom alone. What do you do?

**Answer:** Assist per care plan, retrieve walker, educate resident kindly, ensure path is clear, notify nurse if this is new unsafe behavior, and document. Store assistive devices within reach per facility policy.

### Lesson: Prevention Planning (`prevention-plan`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Implement individualized fall prevention strategies from the care plan.
- Coordinate rounding, toileting, and repositioning to reduce unassisted movement.
- Respond correctly after a fall or near-fall event.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Individualized Prevention
Fall prevention is not one-size-fits-all. Plans may combine:
- Scheduled toileting every two hours
- Bed in lowest position with one rail per policy
- Bed or chair alarm
- Non-skid socks and proper shoes
- Walker within reach; call light in hand
- High-risk signage per facility policy (without shaming resident)
- Increased observation during shift change or sundowning

Consistency matters. Skipping alarms because a resident complains undermines safety—involve nurse for balanced solutions preserving dignity.

##### Proactive Rounding
Purposeful rounds address pain, position, potty, possessions, and proximity (the "4 Ps" or similar models). Proactive toileting reduces urgent attempts to stand alone. Offer fluids on schedule unless fluid restricted. Keep glasses and hearing aids on when tolerated.

During evenings, increase supervision for residents with sundowning or prior night falls.

##### Communication Across the Team
Share fall risks in handoff. If a resident had a near-fall during your shift, report details: time, location, symptoms, assist used. Near-falls are learning opportunities to adjust the plan before injury.

Family education: explain why alarms and assist levels protect their loved one. Enlist family to avoid encouraging unsafe independence against medical orders.

##### After a Fall—Do Not Panic, Do Follow Protocol
1. **Do not move** the resident quickly unless immediate danger (fire). Assess consciousness and breathing.
2. Call nurse; monitor vitals per scope and policy.
3. Look for visible injury but do not assume fine if no bleeding—hip fractures may have subtle signs.
4. Complete incident report factually: time, location, activity, witnesses, equipment in use.
5. Monitor per nursing orders post-fall (neuro checks, vitals series).

Never blame the resident in documentation. Objective facts support quality improvement.

#### KEY POINTS
- Execute care plan interventions consistently every round.
- Scheduled toileting, fluids, and equipment within reach prevent rushing.
- Report near-falls; they predict future injuries.
- Post-fall: assess, notify nurse, monitor, document objectively.
- Engage family as partners without bypassing safety orders.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** A resident with a bed alarm complains it is embarrassing and asks you to leave it off. What is the appropriate response?

**Answer:** Explain the safety purpose with respect, notify nurse to review alternatives (toileting schedule, mat, closer observation), and keep ordered interventions until the nurse changes the plan. Do not unilaterally disable safety devices.

**Question 2:** You find a resident on the floor alert and complaining of hip pain after an unwitnessed fall. What is the first priority?

**Answer:** Stay with resident, call nurse immediately, avoid moving unless trained head-injury or emergency protocol requires, monitor airway and breathing, and follow facility post-fall assessment. Do not lift alone onto bed without nurse guidance.
