### Lesson: Introduction & Learning Objectives (`introduction`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES

- Explain course completion criteria and certificate eligibility.
- Outline major topics: standard precautions, transmission-based precautions, and environmental practices.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Welcome and Purpose
Infections harm vulnerable residents in nursing homes, hospitals, and home care settings every day. As a CNA, you are one of the most important defenses against infection spread because you provide hands-on care—bathing, toileting, feeding, turning, and transporting residents. Germs move on hands, equipment, and clothing when procedures are rushed or skipped.

This course provides evidence-based infection prevention aligned with California CNA continuing education expectations. After completion and passing required assessments, you earn documentation toward your renewal hours. Retain your certificate with hour details for your personal renewal file (keep records four years).

##### Who This Course Serves
Content is written for California CNAs working in skilled nursing facilities, assisted living, memory care, and other healthcare environments. Many learners speak English as a second language. Key ideas are repeated in plain language: clean hands, correct PPE, respect isolation signs, and clean surfaces.

You do not need to memorize every germ name. You need to know **what to do** when a resident has diarrhea, cough, open wounds, or isolation precautions on the door.

##### Completion Criteria
Lessons include reading, knowledge checks, and a final exam. You must meet each lesson's completion rule—view all content and pass required quizzes—to become certificate eligible. Final exam passing score is 80% unless your enrollment states otherwise. Seat time is estimated at 60 minutes total; take the time you need to learn, not just click through.

##### Course Roadmap
**Lesson 1 (this lesson):** Introduction and expectations.

**Lesson 2 — Standard Precautions & Hand Hygiene:** Daily practices for every resident, glove use, and hand washing vs. alcohol gel.

**Lesson 3 — Final Review & Exam:** Pulls together transmission-based precautions (contact, droplet, airborne), healthcare-associated infections (HAIs), N95 basics, sharps safety concepts, and environmental cleaning.

##### Why Infection Control Is Mandatory in California
CDPH requires infection control content in CNA continuing education because outbreaks in long-term care cause hospitalization, death, and survey penalties. Facilities also follow Cal/OSHA requirements for worker protection. When you follow precautions, you protect residents, coworkers, and your ability to keep working during outbreaks.

##### Your Professional Responsibility
Infection control is not optional extra work. Skipping gloves, wearing isolation gowns in the dining room, or coming to work ill with fever and diarrhea can spread disease to dozens of fragile people. If supplies are missing or training is unclear, report to your charge nurse or infection prevention nurse the same shift.

#### KEY POINTS
- CNAs are frontline infection preventionists through daily hands-on care.
- Complete all lessons and pass quizzes/exam for certificate eligibility; keep records four years.
- Focus on actions: hand hygiene, PPE, isolation rules, cleaning, staying home when ill.
- Course aligns with California CNA CE infection control expectations.
- Report inadequate supplies or training—system fixes prevent outbreaks.

### Lesson: Standard Precautions & Hand Hygiene (`standard-precautions`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Apply standard precautions to all residents regardless of diagnosis.
- Perform hand hygiene correctly with soap and water or alcohol-based rub.
- Use gloves and other PPE appropriately without contaminating clean areas.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Standard Precautions Defined
Standard precautions treat **all blood, all body fluids (except sweat), non-intact skin, and mucous membranes** as potentially infectious. You do not wait for a positive culture to wear gloves for peri-care or to wash hands after removing gloves. Standard precautions are the baseline; transmission-based precautions add layers when specific diseases require them.

Body fluids include urine, feces, vomit, saliva with blood, drainage from wounds, and respiratory secretions. Sweat is not considered infectious unless visibly contaminated with blood.

##### Hand Hygiene—Your Strongest Tool
The World Health Organization identifies five moments for hand hygiene in healthcare:
1. Before touching a resident
2. Before clean procedures within your scope
3. After risk of body fluid exposure
4. After touching a resident
5. After touching surroundings near the resident

Alcohol-based hand rub (60–95% alcohol) is effective for most routine care when hands are not visibly dirty. Rub all surfaces until dry (about 20 seconds).

**Soap and water is required when:**
- Hands are visibly soiled
- After caring for residents with **C. difficile** or **norovirus** (spores and viruses not fully killed by alcohol alone)
- After using the restroom
- Before eating

In California facilities, surveyors watch hand hygiene. Build habits: gel when entering room, wash when leaving isolation for C. diff.

##### Gloves and Gown Basics
**Gloves** reduce hand contamination; they do not sterilize your technique. Change gloves between dirty and clean steps on the same resident (peri-care then face cloth). Never wear the same gloves room to room.

**Gowns** protect clothing during heavy contact or when isolation sign requires them. Tie securely, remove without touching outside, discard in linen/waste per policy, then hand hygiene.

**Mask and eye protection** when splashes or sprays possible—suctioning (if in scope), violent vomiting, some wound care.

##### Environmental Cleaning at CNA Level
Wipe high-touch items you use: bed rails, call buttons, gait belts shared between tasks. Follow facility protocols for disinfectant contact time—spray and immediate wipe may be ineffective. Report empty sanitizer dispensers and broken sinks.

##### When You Are Ill
Fever, vomiting, diarrhea, or untreated draining skin lesions may mean you should not provide direct care. Follow occupational health and facility return-to-work policies. Working sick has caused deadly norovirus and influenza outbreaks in California SNFs.

##### C. diff and MRSA—Preview
**C. diff** causes watery diarrhea, often after antibiotics. Spores survive on surfaces and alcohol gel may not remove them from hands—use soap and water and contact precautions.

**MRSA** (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus) spreads by contact. Colonized residents may have no symptoms but still require precautions per facility policy. Never share personal care items without cleaning.

Detailed precaution types are reviewed in the final lesson exam prep.

#### KEY POINTS
- Standard precautions apply to all residents; all blood/body fluids may carry infection.
- Hand hygiene before and after every resident contact; gloves never replace washing.
- Soap and water for C. diff, norovirus, visible soil, and restroom use.
- Change gloves between dirty/clean tasks; do not wear between rooms.
- Stay home per policy when ill with GI or respiratory symptoms.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** You wear gloves for peri-care and plan to apply lotion to resident's arms next. Correct sequence?

**Answer:** Remove and discard gloves, perform hand hygiene, apply lotion with clean hands (or new gloves if policy prefers), then hand hygiene again after care.

**Question 2:** Resident on contact precautions for C. diff asks you to shake hands. Safe approach?

**Answer:** Explain kindly, perform hand hygiene with soap and water after any contact, use gloves for care tasks, and follow contact precautions. Brief hand shake with immediate thorough hand washing may be acceptable if policy allows, but full precautions still apply for care.

### Lesson: Final Review & Exam (`final-review`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Review transmission-based precautions: contact, droplet, and airborne.
- Explain healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) and CNA prevention roles.
- Prepare for the final exam covering N95 basics, sharps safety, and outbreak response.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Transmission-Based Precautions Overview
When standard precautions are not enough, facilities add transmission-based precautions based on how pathogens spread:

**Contact precautions**—for MRSA (per policy), C. diff, draining wounds, multidrug-resistant organisms. Gown and gloves on room entry; dedicated equipment when possible; soap and water emphasis for C. diff; sporicidal cleaning.

**Droplet precautions**—for influenza, COVID-19 per current facility policy, pertussis, mumps. Surgical mask within 6 feet; resident masks during transport if tolerated; limit movement.

**Airborne precautions**—for pulmonary tuberculosis (active), measles, varicella in specific contexts. Negative pressure room; **N95 respirator** for staff; only fit-tested staff enter routinely.

Memorize pairs: **C. diff = contact + soap/water**; **TB = airborne + N95**.

##### N95 Respirator Basics (CNA Level)
Surgical masks block droplets; **N95 respirators** filter small airborne particles. N95 requires medical clearance, fit testing, and seal check each use. Facial hair breaking the seal reduces protection. If you are not fit-tested, tell the nurse before entering airborne isolation—do not substitute a surgical mask.

During shortages, follow facility crisis standards; document training received.

##### Healthcare-Associated Infections (HAIs)
HAIs are infections acquired during healthcare delivery. Common in LTC:
- **UTIs** from indwelling catheters (nursing manages insertion; CNAs provide peri-care and report sediment, odor, fever)
- **Pneumonia** including aspiration from swallowing problems
- **Skin and soft tissue infections** including pressure injuries
- **Gastrointestinal outbreaks** (norovirus, C. diff)

Prevention: hand hygiene, oral care, repositioning, aspiration precautions, early reporting of clusters.

##### Sharps Safety
Needlesticks expose you to bloodborne pathogens (HIV, hepatitis B and C). CNAs may encounter sharps in some settings—never recap needles, do not pass unsheathed sharps hand-to-hand, dispose immediately in puncture-resistant containers. Report sticks immediately to occupational health for post-exposure protocols.

##### Exposure Incidents
If blood or body fluids splash in eyes or mouth, or needlestick occurs:
1. Wash/expose area per protocol
2. Report same shift to supervisor and occupational health
3. Document incident
4. Follow post-exposure testing and treatment timelines—delays reduce prophylaxis effectiveness

California employers must have bloodborne pathogen plans.

##### Outbreak Response
When multiple residents or staff have vomiting/diarrhea or respiratory symptoms:
- Notify infection prevention and charge nurse immediately
- Enforce precautions and hand hygiene
- Restrict group dining and new admissions per direction
- Ill staff must exclude until cleared

##### Exam Tips
Read each scenario for **organism + setting + correct precaution**. Choose nurse notification when supplies, fit testing, or policy gaps appear. Certificate eligibility requires passing the final exam—review key pairs and hand hygiene rules above.

#### KEY POINTS
- Contact, droplet, and airborne precautions add to standard precautions by spread route.
- N95 requires fit testing; TB uses airborne isolation, not droplet rules alone.
- HAIs common in LTC—prevent with hygiene, oral care, positioning, aspiration safety.
- Sharps injuries and splashes need immediate occupational health reporting.
- Clusters trigger outbreak protocols—early communication saves lives.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** Fit-tested CNA cares for resident with active pulmonary TB in negative pressure room. PPE required?

**Answer:** N95 respirator (fit-checked), plus gown/gloves as policy dictates for additional tasks; follow airborne isolation signage; do not use surgical mask alone.

**Question 2:** Four staff and six residents develop vomiting within 48 hours. Best first action?

**Answer:** Notify charge nurse and infection prevention immediately; implement contact/enteric precautions per policy, enforce soap and water hand hygiene, restrict group activities, begin tracking cases—suspect norovirus or similar outbreak until identified.
