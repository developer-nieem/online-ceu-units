## Module: Understanding Dementia (`understanding-dementia`)

### Lesson: Recognizing Early Signs of Cognitive Decline (`recognizing-early-signs`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Identify early behavioral and functional signs of cognitive decline.
- Distinguish normal aging forgetfulness from concerning changes.
- Report observations that may indicate new or worsening dementia.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Normal Aging vs. Concerning Change
Many older adults forget names or lose keys occasionally and remember later. That can be normal aging. Concerning signs are **persistent**, **progressive**, and **interfere with daily life**: getting lost in familiar places, unable to manage medications or finances, repeating questions within minutes, or personality change over weeks.

As a CNA, you see residents daily. Families may not notice gradual decline. Your reports help nurses arrange medical evaluation for reversible causes (infection, dehydration, medication side effects) before assuming permanent dementia.

##### Early Signs to Watch
- **Memory loss** affecting function—missed meals, unpaid bills when previously independent
- **Difficulty with familiar tasks**—cannot set table sequence, trouble dressing in order
- **Language problems**—word-finding, calling objects wrong names
- **Disorientation**—time, place, especially worse at night
- **Poor judgment**—wearing winter coat in summer, giving money to strangers
- **Mood and personality shifts**—new suspicion, apathy, anxiety
- **Withdrawal** from hobbies or social events
- **Misplacing items** in unusual locations (keys in freezer)

Sudden confusion over hours or days suggests **delirium**, not slow dementia—urgent nurse assessment for UTI, pneumonia, stroke, or new drugs.

##### Functional Clues During Care
Early dementia may show as:
- Needing more cues for hygiene steps
- Hiding soiled clothing due to embarrassment
- Resistance to bathing from fear or sensory overload
- Forgetting how to use utensils but still wanting independence

Document specific examples with dates: "Required step-by-step prompting for shirt buttons; independent 2 weeks ago per prior note."

##### Communication With Team and Family
Share observations objectively at handoff. Do not tell families "your mom has Alzheimer's"—that is medical diagnosis. Say "She needed more prompting with meals and seemed confused about lunch time; nurse notified."

Early diagnosis allows treatment of reversible factors, medication review, safety planning, and advance care discussions while resident can participate.

#### KEY POINTS
- Concerning decline is persistent, progressive, and affects daily function.
- Sudden confusion suggests delirium—report immediately for medical causes.
- CNAs notice functional changes during ADLs before office visits occur.
- Document specific comparisons over time, not vague "confused."
- Report to nurse; do not diagnose dementia.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** A resident forgets a grandchild's name once but remembers later and manages grooming independently. Concern level?

**Answer:** Low concern if isolated and function intact—may be normal aging. Continue monitoring and report pattern if worsens or affects care.

**Question 2:** Over two days, a resident becomes acutely confused, falls, and has foul-smelling urine. Likely issue?

**Answer:** Possible delirium from UTI or other acute illness—not slow dementia progression alone. Report immediately for nurse assessment, vitals, and possible testing.

### Lesson: Types and Stages of Dementia (`types-and-stages`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Name common types of dementia relevant to long-term care.
- Describe general stage characteristics and care implications.
- Adjust expectations and safety plans by stage without labeling residents harshly.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### What Is Dementia?
Dementia is a syndrome of cognitive decline severe enough to interfere with independence. It is not one disease. Many conditions cause dementia symptoms. Medical providers diagnose type and stage; CNAs apply person-centered care based on function and behavior.

##### Common Types in Facilities
**Alzheimer's disease:** Most common; gradual memory loss, difficulty learning new info, later language and spatial problems.

**Vascular dementia:** Stepwise decline after strokes or vascular disease; may affect judgment and walking early.

**Lewy body dementia:** Fluctuating alertness, visual hallucinations, parkinson-like movement, sensitive to some medications.

**Frontotemporal dementia:** Personality and behavior changes early; memory may be preserved initially.

**Mixed dementia:** Combination common in elders.

Knowing type helps anticipate risks—Lewy body may worsen with certain antipsychotics; vascular may have sudden changes.

##### Stages (General Framework)
**Early:** Some independence; needs reminders; driving/finances unsafe often.

**Middle:** More assistance with ADLs; wandering, repetition, sundowning may appear; still may have moments of clarity.

**Late:** Major dependence for eating, mobility, communication; high risk skin breakdown and infections; may still respond to touch, music, calm voice.

Stages are not labels to limit dignity. A "late-stage" resident still deserves pain management, mouth care, and respectful communication.

##### Care Implications by Stage
Early: encourage choices and routines; simplify instructions.
Middle: one-step commands, visual cues, safe wandering paths, consistent caregivers when possible.
Late: slow gentle care, oral hygiene, repositioning, observe nonverbal pain, involve family in comfort rituals.

California facilities use care plans updated with nursing—follow mobility, nutrition, and behavior interventions listed.

#### KEY POINTS
- Dementia has multiple types; diagnosis is medical, care is functional and person-centered.
- Alzheimer's is most common; vascular, Lewy body, and frontotemporal have distinct patterns.
- Stages guide assistance level—not worth or dignity.
- Match communication and safety to stage while treating each person individually.
- Follow updated care plans for behaviors and risks.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** Resident has vivid visual hallucinations and stiff shuffling gait. Nurse mentions Lewy body dementia. Special caution?

**Answer:** Avoid giving medications outside orders especially sedating antipsychotics without careful medical oversight; monitor falls; expect alertness fluctuations; provide calm reassurance during hallucinations without arguing reality unless safety requires redirection per plan.

**Question 2:** Late-stage resident no longer speaks but grimaces during turns. Appropriate pain approach?

**Answer:** Assume pain possible; use gentle slow handling, pain scale tools for nonverbal residents per facility protocol, report to nurse for assessment and orders, document behaviors during care.

### Lesson: Person-Centered Care Principles (`person-centered-care`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Define person-centered dementia care and why it improves outcomes.
- Apply validation and redirection techniques within CNA scope.
- Honor resident history, preferences, and remaining abilities.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Beyond Task Completion
Person-centered care sees the whole person—life story, culture, preferences—not only dementia diagnosis. Tasks done "to" residents increase resistance; care done "with" residents builds cooperation and dignity.

Learn preferences: favorite music, past occupation, morning vs. evening bath, foods they love or avoid for cultural reasons. Use a "life story" sheet if facility provides one.

##### Validation Therapy Concepts
Validation, developed for dementia communication, suggests many behaviors express unmet needs or past emotions. Instead of correcting aggressively:
- **Validate feelings:** "You seem worried about missing the bus."
- **Explore gently:** "Tell me about where you need to go."
- **Redirect safely:** "Let's walk to the dining room together; lunch is ready."

Arguing ("You're in a nursing home, not 1952") often escalates agitation.

##### Preserved Abilities
Residents may retain long-term memories, music appreciation, procedural memory for rituals, and emotional sensitivity to tone. Use strengths: let them fold warm laundry, polish tables, or hold a baby doll if therapeutic and policy allows.

##### Consistency and Teamwork
Consistent approaches among CNAs reduce confusion. Share what works in handoff: "Soft music helps before shower." Avoid negative labels in charts—describe triggers instead.

##### Rights and Restraint-Free Culture
Person-centered care minimizes chemical and physical restraints. Wandering may need secure units or bracelets per policy—not punishment. Report care that uses restraint for staff convenience.

#### KEY POINTS
- Person-centered care honors history, preferences, and remaining strengths.
- Validate feelings before redirection; arguing increases agitation.
- Use meaningful activities aligned with past roles when safe.
- Share successful approaches in handoff; avoid stigmatizing labels.
- Support restraint-minimizing policies; advocate for dignity.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** Resident packs clothes daily saying she must get home to her mother (deceased 40 years). Best response?

**Answer:** Validate emotion: "You miss your mother." Redirect to comforting activity or walk, ensure secure environment if elopement risk, notify nurse if new or escalating, document trigger and effective interventions.

**Question 2:** Coworker yells "I already told you!" when resident repeats question. Your action?

**Answer:** Model calm repetition with simple answers, support resident dignity, debrief coworker on person-centered communication, notify supervisor if pattern constitutes verbal abuse.

## Module: Daily Care Skills (`daily-care`)

### Lesson: Bathing and Hygiene Strategies (`bathing-and-hygiene`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Plan bathing approaches that reduce fear and agitation in dementia.
- Apply skin-friendly hygiene for older adults with sensitivity.
- Document hygiene refusals and skin findings objectively.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Why Bathing Triggers Distress
Bathing involves cold air, water noise, nudity, and touch—overwhelming when brain cannot process sensations. Past trauma or modesty may heighten fear. Goal is clean, intact skin with minimal distress—not forcing daily full baths when partial hygiene meets need.

##### Preparation Steps
- Warm room, towels ready, non-slip mats
- Explain each step calmly; same caregiver when possible
- Offer choices: sponge bath vs. shower, morning vs. afternoon
- Play familiar music; cover mirror if it confuses
- Have clothing ready before starting to reduce exposure time

##### Techniques During Bath
- **One step at a time:** "We will wash your face now."
- **Distraction:** chat about neutral topics
- **Hand-under-hand guidance** for resident participation
- **Stop if escalating:** try later or split care (face, peri, feet on different schedule)

Never leave wet resident unattended—fall and chilling risks.

##### Skin and Dementia
Dry skin itches and triggers scratching. Use mild soaps, pat dry, moisturize per care plan. Inspect folds, feet, and peri areas for redness or fungal rash. Report open areas immediately.

For incontinence, timely changes prevent dermatitis and UTIs. Use barrier creams per orders.

##### Refusals and Care Plans
If resident refuses, document, try alternative timing, notify nurse if skin odor or infection risk. Forced bathing can be trauma and rights concern unless safety/medical orders specify supervised approach with interdisciplinary plan.

#### KEY POINTS
- Bath distress is sensory and emotional—prepare environment and offer choices.
- Use calm pacing, distraction, and hand-under-hand participation.
- Stop and retry later if agitation rises; split hygiene tasks when needed.
- Protect skin with gentle products and timely incontinence care.
- Document refusals and skin findings; involve nurse for persistent refusal.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** Resident screams and strikes during shower. First attempt failed. Next step?

**Answer:** Stop shower, ensure safety, warm and cover resident, notify nurse, document triggers, try sponge bath of face/peri/feet later per revised plan. Consider pain, UTI, or fear as causes.

**Question 2:** You notice scaly red rash in abdominal folds during bath. Action?

**Answer:** Document location and appearance, apply treatment per care plan if ordered or notify nurse for assessment and new orders, keep area clean and dry, continue monitoring.

### Lesson: Nutrition and Hydration Support (`nutrition-support`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Support adequate food and fluid intake for residents with dementia.
- Apply safe feeding assistance techniques for swallowing risk.
- Recognize weight loss and dehydration signs early.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Nutrition Challenges in Dementia
Residents may forget to eat, lose interest in food, wander during meals, or have difficulty using utensils. Medications and depression affect appetite. Poor intake leads to weight loss, weakness, falls, and delayed healing.

CNAs are central to meal setup, cueing, and observation of intake—key data for dietitian and nursing.

##### Mealtime Support Strategies
- **Quiet environment:** reduce TV noise; consistent seating
- **Visual cues:** colored plates for contrast; open containers
- **Finger foods** when utensils frustrate—check diet texture orders
- **Cueing:** "Pick up your sandwich." Model eating
- **Small frequent meals** if large plates overwhelm
- **Hydration rounds** between meals—offer cups with handles, straws if safe per speech therapy

Record intake percentages accurately—"ate 50% lunch" not "ate well."

##### Texture and Swallowing Safety
Follow diet orders: regular, mechanical soft, pureed, thickened liquids. Do not thin liquids because resident prefers it—aspiration pneumonia risk. If coughing during meals, stop feeding, notify nurse, request speech therapy swallow eval.

Sit upright 90 degrees during and 30 minutes after meals when ordered. No rushed feeding.

##### Wandering and Sundowning at Meals
Schedule extra staff at dinner if sundowning peaks. Offer snacks for wanderers. Calm redirection back to table; validate then guide.

##### Monitoring Weight and Hydration
Report:
- Clothes looser, cheeks hollow
- Dark urine, dry mouth, dizziness
- Edema vs. dehydration confusion—nurse assesses

Offer fluids with preferred flavors when allowed. Track I&O if assigned.

#### KEY POINTS
- Dementia meal problems are common; accurate intake documentation drives interventions.
- Use contrast plates, finger foods, cueing, and quiet dining environments.
- Never alter thickened liquid or texture orders without nurse/dietitian direction.
- Upright positioning and slow feeding reduce aspiration risk.
- Report weight loss and dehydration signs early.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** Resident with pureed diet and nectar-thick liquids requests regular water. What do you do?

**Answer:** Do not give thin water—aspiration risk. Offer nectar-thick fluids per orders, notify nurse if resident refuses fluids for swallow evaluation, document intake.

**Question 2:** Resident eats only sweets and skips protein at every meal for a week. Concern?

**Answer:** Report pattern to nurse and dietitian; poor protein intake risks muscle loss and wounds. Document specific intake; interdisciplinary plan may need supplements or preferred protein options.

## Module: Course Assessment (`assessment`)

### Lesson: Knowledge Check (`knowledge-check`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Synthesize dementia recognition, staging, person-centered care, and daily care skills.
- Apply safe responses to wandering, sundowning, and mealtime risk scenarios.
- Prepare for final course assessment.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Integrated Dementia Care Model
Effective CNA dementia care combines:
- **Early recognition** and delirium differentiation
- **Type/stage awareness** without stereotyping
- **Person-centered communication**—validation, calm tone, one-step instructions
- **Daily care adaptation** for bathing and nutrition
- **Safety** for wandering, falls, elopement, and aspiration

California long-term care serves growing numbers of residents with dementia. Your skill reduces psychotropic overuse, injuries, and distress.

##### Wandering and Elopement
Wandering is not always aimless—it may express boredom or unmet toileting needs. Provide safe wandering paths, regular toileting, activities, and monitoring per care plan. Elopement risk requires alarms, secure units, and identification per policy. Never restrain without proper orders.

##### Sundowning
Increased confusion, agitation, and pacing late afternoon/evening. Strategies: daylight exposure, reduce caffeine late day, calm routines, reduce noise, increase staffing if possible, pain and toileting checks before peak hours.

##### Documentation That Helps the Team
Note triggers ("bath after loud TV"), effective interventions ("country music reduced yelling"), intake percentages, skin changes, and new behaviors with onset time. Avoid labels like "combative" without describing antecedents.

##### Exam Readiness
Expect scenarios on bathing refusals, meal safety, validation vs. arguing, reporting delirium, and rights-based care. Choose responses that protect dignity, safety, and nurse involvement.

#### KEY POINTS
- Combine observation, person-centered methods, and safety across all shifts.
- Wandering needs meaningful activity and safe environments—not punishment.
- Sundowning management includes routine, environment, and unmet needs checks.
- Objective trigger-based documentation improves care plans.
- Final assessment rewards dignity-centered, practical CNA actions.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** 17:00 daily, resident paces halls, tries exit doors, and pushes staff. Two appropriate interventions?

**Answer:** Implement sundowning plan: scheduled toileting, snack, calming activity, reduce overstimulation, ensure elopement precautions per care plan, validate feelings, notify nurse if new or escalating for medication/medical review.

**Question 2:** Resident chokes lightly on lunch, coughs, watery eyes. Next actions?

**Answer:** Stop feeding, encourage cough if effective, notify nurse immediately, monitor respiratory status per orders, request swallow evaluation, document event and diet order compliance. Do not resume regular diet without clearance.
