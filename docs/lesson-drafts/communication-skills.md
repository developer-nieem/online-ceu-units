## Module: Therapeutic Communication (`therapeutic-comm`)

### Lesson: Active Listening Skills (`active-listening`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Explain why active listening improves trust and safety during resident care.
- Use three listening techniques that help residents feel heard and respected.
- Recognize when to pause care tasks and give full attention to a resident.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Why Listening Matters in Long-Term Care
As a CNA, you spend more direct time with residents than almost any other team member. Residents may share fears, pain, or personal stories during routine care. When you listen well, you help them feel valued. Good listening also helps you catch important changes in mood, appetite, or pain that should be reported to the nurse.

Active listening means you focus on the speaker. You are not just waiting for your turn to talk. You show interest with your eyes, body position, and short responses. Many residents have hearing loss, memory problems, or trouble finding words. Give them extra time. Do not finish their sentences unless they ask for help.

##### Core Techniques You Can Use Every Shift
**Give your full attention.** Stop what you are doing when a resident wants to talk, if it is safe to pause. Face the person. Make eye contact at their level if they are in a bed or wheelchair. Nod or say short phrases like "I hear you" or "Tell me more."

**Reflect what you heard.** Repeat the main idea in your own words. For example: "It sounds like you are worried about your daughter's visit." This shows you understood. It also gives the resident a chance to correct you if needed.

**Ask open questions.** Questions that start with "how," "what," or "tell me about" invite more detail than yes-or-no questions. "How did you sleep?" opens more conversation than "Did you sleep okay?"

**Avoid rushing to fix.** Residents often want to be heard before they want a solution. If someone shares a concern, listen first. Then say, "Would you like me to tell the nurse?" or "What would help right now?"

##### Listening During Care Tasks
Care tasks are busy. You can still listen while you work if you explain what you are doing and check comfort often. Example: "I am going to wash your back now. Is the water temperature okay?" If a resident becomes upset during care, slow down. Lower your voice. Listening during bath or toileting protects dignity and reduces resistance.

##### When to Report What You Hear
You are not a counselor, but you are an important observer. Report to the nurse when a resident talks about:
- New or worsening pain
- Not wanting to eat or drink
- Fear of another resident or staff member
- Thoughts of harming themselves
- Confusion that is new today

Document facts objectively: what the resident said, when, and what you did (for example, notified the charge nurse at 14:30).

#### KEY POINTS
- Active listening builds trust and helps you notice changes in condition.
- Use eye contact, reflection, and open questions; allow extra time for older adults.
- Pause when safe; do not multitask important emotional conversations.
- Listen first; offer solutions or nurse referral after the resident feels heard.
- Report safety concerns and significant mood or health changes promptly.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** Mrs. Lopez tells you during morning care, "Nobody listens to me in this place." She looks tearful. What is your best first response as a CNA?

**Answer:** Stop the task if safe, face her, and use active listening: "I want to hear you, Mrs. Lopez. Can you tell me what happened?" Reflect her concern, avoid arguing or defending the facility, and report her distress to the nurse. Document what she said and your notification.

**Question 2:** A resident with mild dementia repeats the same story three times in one shift. What should you do?

**Answer:** Listen patiently each time without correcting harshly. Short, kind responses ("That sounds like a special memory") validate the person. If repetition is new or sudden, report to the nurse—it may signal infection, pain, or medication change.

### Lesson: Nonverbal Communication (`nonverbal-cues`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Identify nonverbal signals that show pain, anxiety, or discomfort.
- Adjust your own body language to support calm, respectful care.
- Describe how cultural background may affect nonverbal communication.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### What Nonverbal Communication Includes
Words are only part of a message. Tone of voice, facial expression, posture, touch, and personal space also send strong signals. Residents read your nonverbal cues to decide if they feel safe. A rushed sigh, frown, or hard grip on an arm can cause fear or anger—even when your words are kind.

Watch the resident's whole body, not only their face. Grimacing, guarding an area, pulling away, clenched fists, or restless movement may mean pain or anxiety. Some residents cannot put pain into words because of stroke, dementia, or language barriers. Your observation helps the team treat problems early.

##### Your Nonverbal Messages During Care
**Approach calmly.** Knock before entering. Introduce yourself even if you have met the resident before. Smile when appropriate. Stand where they can see you; avoid surprising them from behind.

**Respect personal space.** Move slowly. Explain before touch: "I need to help you with your shirt." Wait for a nod or verbal okay when possible. For residents who dislike touch, offer choices: "Would you like to hold the washcloth yourself?"

**Match your tone to the situation.** Soft, steady speech calms agitation. A loud or sharp tone increases confusion in residents with dementia. Lower environmental noise when having important conversations.

**Touch with purpose.** Gentle, brief touch on a hand or shoulder can comfort if the resident accepts touch. Never use touch to force compliance. Stop if the person pulls away.

##### Culture and Nonverbal Differences
California long-term care serves people from many backgrounds. Eye contact, touch, and distance rules differ by culture. Some residents avoid direct eye contact with staff as a sign of respect. Others may prefer family involvement in decisions. When unsure, ask politely: "How would you like me to help you today?" Involve the charge nurse or social services for ongoing communication needs. Use interpreter services for health discussions—do not rely on family alone unless the resident requests it.

##### Working With Residents Who Cannot Speak Clearly
Use picture boards, gestures, and yes/no questions. Watch breathing rate, moaning, and body tension during turns and transfers. Compare today to their usual pattern. Share specific observations: "During transfer, Mr. Kim winced and guarded his right hip."

#### KEY POINTS
- Nonverbal cues often reveal pain and emotion before words do.
- Calm approach, explained touch, and steady tone reduce fear and resistance.
- Respect cultural differences in eye contact, space, and family roles.
- Document specific behaviors, not labels like "difficult."
- Match interventions to what the resident's body is telling you.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** While feeding Mr. Patel, he turns his head away, purses his lips, and pushes the spoon aside without speaking. What might this mean, and what should you do?

**Answer:** These nonverbal cues may mean refusal, fullness, pain, or dislike of the food. Stop feeding. Ask simple yes/no questions. Check oral comfort and positioning. Report continued refusal or signs of swallowing difficulty to the nurse. Document intake and behaviors objectively.

**Question 2:** You feel rushed and realize you have been speaking quickly and standing over a resident in bed. How can you adjust?

**Answer:** Slow down, lower your voice, move to eye level, and explain the next step. Apologize if you seemed hurried. Nonverbal repair restores trust and often makes care faster because the resident cooperates.

## Module: Team Communication (`team-comm`)

### Lesson: Shift Handoffs (`handoffs`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Give a clear, organized handoff using resident-focused information.
- Report changes, safety risks, and unfinished tasks to the oncoming CNA or nurse.
- Avoid common handoff errors that affect resident safety.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Purpose of Handoffs
A handoff (also called report or change-of-shift report) transfers important information from one caregiver to another. Poor handoffs cause missed treatments, falls, and medication errors. As a CNA, your handoff includes what you saw and did—not medical orders you cannot carry out.

Handoffs happen at shift change, breaks, and when a resident transfers to another unit. Treat every handoff as a safety step, even when the floor feels busy.

##### What to Include for Each Resident
Use a consistent order so nothing is skipped. For each assigned resident, share:
- **Identification and code status** (as allowed by facility policy)
- **Recent changes** in behavior, mobility, skin, intake, output, or pain
- **Tasks completed** (bath, meals, toileting, ambulation)
- **Tasks still needed** (PM care, repositioning schedule, fluids)
- **Safety concerns** (fall risk, wandering, aggression triggers, equipment issues)
- **Family or physician visits** and any concerns raised

Use facts and times. Example: "Assist of two for transfers per care plan. Repositioned at 10:00, 13:00, and 16:00. Right heel redness noted—nurse aware, dressing in place."

##### SBAR for Urgent Issues
When reporting a new problem to the nurse, SBAR helps:
- **Situation:** What is happening right now?
- **Background:** What is relevant history today?
- **Assessment:** What did you observe (vitals if taken, behavior, skin)?
- **Recommendation/Request:** What do you need from the nurse?

Example: "Situation: Mr. Davis fell in bathroom. Background: Fall risk, new weakness this shift. Assessment: Alert, small abrasion left knee, vitals stable per nurse check. Request: Please assess and document per protocol."

##### Handoff Etiquette and Privacy
Give handoff in a private area when possible. Lower your voice in hallways. Do not discuss residents in elevators or dining areas. Read back critical details for high-risk residents: "Confirming—bed alarm on for room 12B, last void at 15:45."

If you receive unclear information, ask before starting care. Never assume a task was done unless it was reported.

#### KEY POINTS
- Structured handoffs prevent missed care and safety events.
- Report observations, completed care, pending tasks, and risks.
- Use SBAR for urgent nurse communication.
- Protect privacy; use private spaces and professional tone.
- Clarify unclear handoffs before providing care.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** Outgoing CNA says, "Room 8 is fine." What should you ask before starting your shift?

**Answer:** Ask for specifics: mobility level, meals and fluids, toileting, skin issues, pain, behavior changes, scheduled treatments, and fall precautions. "Fine" is not enough for safe care.

**Question 2:** You notice the previous shift did not reposition a resident on a two-hour schedule. The outgoing CNA is already gone. What do you do?

**Answer:** Reposition the resident now per care plan, inspect skin, notify the charge nurse of the gap and any skin changes, and document what you found and actions taken. Do not ignore a missed safety task.

### Lesson: Family Interactions (`family-interactions`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Communicate with families in a professional, empathetic way within CNA scope.
- Set appropriate boundaries while supporting family involvement.
- Escalate family concerns or conflicts to the nurse or supervisor.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Families as Partners in Care
Family members often know a resident's preferences, routines, and triggers. They may feel guilt, grief, or stress about placement. Your calm, respectful communication helps them trust the care team. You do not share medical diagnoses or care plan changes unless the nurse authorizes you to do so. When in doubt, say, "Let me get the nurse to discuss that with you."

##### Starting Conversations Well
Greet family by name when possible. Introduce yourself and your role. Example: "Good morning, I am Ana, the CNA caring for your mother today." Offer small, factual updates within scope: "She ate about half of breakfast and participated in morning hygiene." Avoid speculation or blame.

Listen to concerns without becoming defensive. Reflect feelings: "I can see you are worried about her weight." Then involve the nurse for clinical questions.

##### Common Challenges
**Conflicting instructions.** If a family member asks you to do something outside the care plan (extra food for a restricted diet, leaving bed alarm off), explain kindly that you must follow the nurse's orders. Notify the charge nurse immediately.

**Frequent or disruptive visiting.** Follow facility visiting policies. If visits interfere with care or rest, coordinate with the nurse or social services—not confrontation alone.

**Cultural and language needs.** Offer interpreter services for important discussions. Do not use children as interpreters for sensitive health topics unless the family prefers it and policy allows.

##### Boundaries That Protect Everyone
Do not accept gifts of significant value or share personal contact information. Do not make promises you cannot keep ("She will definitely walk again"). Do not criticize other staff in front of family. Document significant family interactions and concerns per facility policy.

#### KEY POINTS
- Families provide valuable preference information; stay within CNA scope.
- Use empathy, facts, and nurse referral for clinical topics.
- Never bypass care plans because of family requests—escalate conflicts.
- Maintain professional boundaries; document significant concerns.
- Use interpreter services for clear, ethical communication.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** A daughter insists you give her father regular soda although his care plan lists thickened liquids only. What do you do?

**Answer:** Politely refuse the unsafe request, explain you must follow the care plan for swallowing safety, and notify the charge nurse immediately so the nurse can discuss the plan with the family.

**Question 2:** A family member becomes loud and angry in the hallway about wait times for assistance. What is your best response?

**Answer:** Stay calm, acknowledge feelings ("I understand you are frustrated"), ensure the resident's immediate needs are met if safe, and request supervisor or nurse support. Do not argue in public; move toward privacy and follow facility violence prevention policy if threats occur.
