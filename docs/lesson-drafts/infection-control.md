## Module: Prevention Basics (`prevention-basics`)

### Lesson: Chain of Infection (`chain-of-infection`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Describe the six links in the chain of infection.
- Explain how breaking any link prevents disease spread in facilities.
- Apply chain-of-infection thinking to common CNA care tasks.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Why the Chain Matters
Infections spread when six conditions line up: a pathogen (germ), reservoir (where it lives), portal of exit, mode of transmission, portal of entry, and a susceptible host. In nursing homes, vulnerable hosts are everywhere—older adults with chronic illness, wounds, catheters, and weakened immunity. CNAs break the chain every shift through hygiene, PPE, and cleaning.

Thinking in "links" helps you decide what action stops an outbreak: hand hygiene after touching a reservoir, gloves before contact with body fluids, isolating contagious residents, or boosting host defenses with nutrition and vaccination per team plan.

##### The Six Links Explained
**1. Infectious agent (pathogen):** Bacteria (MRSA, C. diff), viruses (influenza, COVID-19, norovirus), fungi. Each has different survival on surfaces and different treatments.

**2. Reservoir:** Human body, contaminated surfaces, equipment, food, water. In facilities, shared wheelchairs, glucometers, and bathroom fixtures can harbor germs if not cleaned.

**3. Portal of exit:** Pathogen leaves the body—respiratory droplets, stool, blood, broken skin, drainage.

**4. Mode of transmission:** Contact (direct touch or indirect via surfaces), droplet (cough/sneeze within short range), airborne (tiny particles that linger, e.g., tuberculosis under special conditions), vehicle (food/water), vector (less common in SNFs).

**5. Portal of entry:** Mouth, nose, eyes, non-intact skin, mucous membranes, invasive devices.

**6. Susceptible host:** Resident or staff with age, illness, medications, or breaks in skin defenses.

Break **any** link to stop spread. Most CNA actions target transmission and portals of entry.

##### Practical Examples for CNAs
- **C. diff:** Spores in stool contaminate surfaces; enter via mouth after poor hand hygiene with soap and water (alcohol gel alone is insufficient for C. diff spores).
- **Influenza:** Exits via cough; transmits by droplets; enters via nose/mouth—mask and hand hygiene reduce spread.
- **MRSA:** Skin colonization; contact transmission via hands and equipment—gloves, hand hygiene, dedicated equipment when possible.

##### Standard Precautions Apply to All
Treat all blood and body fluids as potentially infectious. This universal approach breaks transmission before you know the diagnosis.

#### KEY POINTS
- Infection needs pathogen, reservoir, exit, transmission, entry, and susceptible host.
- CNAs break transmission and entry links most often.
- C. diff requires soap and water hand washing; spores survive alcohol gel.
- Standard precautions treat all residents as potentially infectious.
- Shared equipment demands cleaning between residents.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** A resident with diarrhea is not yet diagnosed. Which link do gloves and hand hygiene primarily break?

**Answer:** They break contact transmission (mode) and portal of entry by keeping your hands from carrying pathogens to yourself, other residents, or surfaces.

**Question 2:** Norovirus spreads rapidly on a unit. Name three chain-breaking actions CNAs can take.

**Answer:** Strict hand hygiene with soap and water, isolate symptomatic residents per policy, clean high-touch surfaces with approved disinfectant, use PPE for contact with vomit/stool, restrict ill staff from work, and enforce dedicated equipment or thorough disinfection between uses.

### Lesson: Hand Hygiene in Practice (`hand-hygiene`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Perform hand washing and alcohol-based hand rub correctly.
- Identify when soap and water is required instead of gel.
- Integrate hand hygiene into gloving and care sequencing.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### When to Clean Hands (WHO "Moments")
Perform hand hygiene:
- Before touching a resident
- Before clean/aseptic procedures (per scope)
- After body fluid exposure risk
- After touching a resident
- After touching resident surroundings
- After removing gloves

Missed moments cause most healthcare-associated infections (HAIs). Gloves do not replace hand hygiene—hands contaminate under glove edges.

##### Soap and Water Technique
1. Wet hands with water
2. Apply soap; lather all surfaces including thumbs, backs, fingertips, under nails—at least 20 seconds
3. Rinse thoroughly
4. Dry with disposable towel; use towel to turn off faucet if not hands-free

**Required when:** hands visibly soiled, after caring for residents with C. diff or norovirus, after using toilet, before eating, after exposure to spores.

##### Alcohol-Based Hand Rub (ABHR)
Use facility-approved gel/foam with 60–95% alcohol when hands not soiled:
- Apply palm to palm, cover all surfaces until dry (about 20 seconds)
- Do not wipe off before dry

ABHR is faster and effective for most routine care except spore-forming organisms.

##### Glove Sequence
Apply gloves when contact with blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, non-intact skin, or contaminated items is expected. Remove gloves before touching clean areas (keyboard, phone, next resident). Hand hygiene immediately after glove removal.

Double-gloving is not routine unless policy specifies (some wound care).

##### Barriers CNAs Face—Solutions
- **No sink nearby:** Carry ABHR; plan care to access sinks for C. diff cases
- **Skin dryness:** use facility moisturizers compatible with skin integrity programs
- **Rush:** build hygiene into room entry ritual—gel before and after every resident

California CDPH surveys observe hand hygiene. Your habits protect licenses and lives.

#### KEY POINTS
- Use WHO five moments; gloves never replace hand hygiene.
- Soap and water for visible soil, C. diff, norovirus, and spores.
- ABHR covers all hand surfaces until completely dry.
- Hand hygiene after every glove removal, every time.
- Plan care to overcome sink distance and busy-shift barriers.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** You removed gloves after peri-care and need to document at the hallway computer. What must you do first?

**Answer:** Perform hand hygiene before touching the computer to avoid contaminating shared equipment and breaking the chain to other residents.

**Question 2:** After caring for a resident with known C. diff diarrhea, which method is appropriate?

**Answer:** Wash hands with soap and water. ABHR alone does not reliably remove C. diff spores. Follow contact precautions and facility cleaning protocols.

## Module: PPE & Precautions (`ppe-and-precautions`)

### Lesson: PPE Selection and Use (`ppe-selection`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Select appropriate PPE for standard and transmission-based precautions.
- Demonstrate correct donning and doffing sequence to prevent self-contamination.
- Describe N95 respirator basics at CNA support level.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Types of PPE
- **Gloves:** contact with body fluids, non-intact skin, contaminated items
- **Gown:** when clothing may be soiled by splashes or extensive contact (isolation, C. diff outbreaks per policy)
- **Mask/surgical mask:** droplet precautions, resident cough, splashes to face
- **Eye protection:** risk of splash to mucous membranes
- **N95 respirator:** airborne precautions (e.g., tuberculosis when active and airborne isolation ordered)—requires fit testing and medical clearance for staff who wear them

CNAs must be trained on facility PPE policies. If unsure which level applies, ask the nurse before entering room.

##### Donning Order (General CDC Approach)
1. Hand hygiene
2. Gown (if needed)
3. Mask or respirator
4. Eye protection (if needed)
5. Gloves (extend over gown cuffs)

##### Doffing—Highest Risk Step
Removing contaminated PPE causes many exposures. Typical sequence:
1. Gloves first—peel off avoiding outside touch; hand hygiene
2. Eye protection if used—hand hygiene
3. Gown—roll inside out; hand hygiene
4. Mask/respirator last—hand hygiene again

Discard in appropriate waste containers. Never wear isolation gown or gloves in hallways or break rooms.

##### Standard vs. Transmission-Based
**Standard precautions:** all residents, all times for risk tasks.
**Contact:** gown and gloves for C. diff, MRSA contact isolation, draining wounds—often dedicated equipment.
**Droplet:** surgical mask within 6 feet for influenza, COVID per current policy, pertussis.
**Airborne:** N95 for TB (negative pressure room), measles—only enter if trained and assigned.

California facilities update policies with CDPH and CDC guidance during outbreaks—read daily safety huddles.

##### Skin and Respiratory Protection Limits
If N95 not fit-tested, you may not substitute with surgical mask for airborne diseases. Notify nurse for reassignment. Report inadequate PPE supply immediately—working without protection harms you and residents.

#### KEY POINTS
- Match PPE to precaution type: standard, contact, droplet, airborne.
- Don before entry; doff carefully with hand hygiene between steps.
- Gloves and gowns stay in isolation room—not worn in common areas.
- N95 requires fit testing; surgical masks do not replace N95 for airborne isolation.
- Report PPE shortages and training gaps promptly.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** You enter a droplet precaution room without a mask to "just grab the call light." Is this acceptable?

**Answer:** No. Don surgical mask (and other required PPE) before entering for any reason. Short visits still permit droplet spread.

**Question 2:** After doffing a contaminated gown, your hands touch the gown exterior accidentally. Next step?

**Answer:** Stop, remove gloves if also contaminated, perform hand hygiene, and redon PPE if re-entering. Report near-exposure per occupational health policy if significant splash occurred.

### Lesson: Isolation Precautions (`isolation-precautions`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Explain contact, droplet, and airborne isolation for common pathogens.
- Apply signage and room procedures to reduce HAI transmission.
- Support environmental cleaning and resident dignity during isolation.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Purpose of Isolation
Isolation precautions reduce spread of organisms that standard precautions alone cannot contain. Orders come from infection prevention and nursing based on diagnosis and symptoms. Respect signs on doors—they list required PPE and special instructions.

##### Contact Precautions
Used for MRSA colonization or infection (policy-dependent), C. diff, draining wounds not contained, some multidrug-resistant organisms.
- Gown and gloves on room entry
- Dedicated equipment when possible (BP cuff, glucometer)
- Soap and water hand hygiene emphasis for C. diff
- Terminal cleaning after discharge or outbreak rules

CNAs provide full care but minimize unnecessary items leaving room. Bag and wipe equipment per policy.

##### Droplet Precautions
Used for influenza, COVID-19 per current facility policy, pertussis, mumps, rubella in some cases.
- Surgical mask within 6 feet of resident
- Resident should wear mask during transport if tolerated
- Door may stay open depending on policy; still limit movement

##### Airborne Precautions
Used for suspected or confirmed pulmonary TB (active), measles, varicella in immunocompromised contexts per policy.
- Negative pressure airborne infection isolation room (AIIR)
- N95 respirator for staff
- Limit transport; surgical mask on resident during necessary transport

CNAs without fit testing must not provide routine care in airborne rooms unless alternative assigned staff available—coordinate with nurse.

##### Environmental Cleaning
Housekeeping follows protocols; CNAs wipe high-touch surfaces during care when spills occur. Use EPA-registered disinfectants per label and facility policy. C. diff requires sporicidal agents approved by infection prevention.

##### Dignity and Psychosocial Needs
Isolation can feel punishing. Explain reasons simply. Increase visits when safe, offer call bell reassurance, coordinate activities, and ensure meals and toileting are not delayed because staff avoid room.

#### KEY POINTS
- Contact, droplet, and airborne precautions match how pathogens spread.
- Follow door signs for PPE every entry; C. diff needs soap and water and sporicidal cleaning.
- Dedicated equipment reduces cross-contamination in contact isolation.
- Airborne isolation requires N95 and negative pressure rooms.
- Maintain dignity—do not neglect isolated residents.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** Resident with MRSA wound drainage in contact isolation needs wheelchair trip to dining room. Plan?

**Answer:** Follow facility policy—often postpone group dining during active draining infection or use dedicated transport with PPE, covered wound, and surface cleaning. Coordinate with nurse and infection prevention; do not bypass precautions for convenience.

**Question 2:** C. diff room sign requires gown, gloves, and soap and water. You see a coworker using only ABHR when leaving. Action?

**Answer:** Educate immediately on C. diff spore risk, perform correct hand washing, notify infection prevention or supervisor, and reinforce team compliance to prevent outbreak spread.

## Module: Final Review (`assessment`)

### Lesson: Infection Control Quiz (`final-quiz`)

#### LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Review mandatory infection control topics for California CNAs.
- Apply precautions to multi-pathogen scenarios.
- Prepare for final course examination.

#### MAIN CONTENT

##### Comprehensive Review
California CNAs must complete infection control continuing education because HAIs cause hospitalization, suffering, and death in long-term care. Core competencies:
- Chain of infection and breaking transmission
- Hand hygiene moments and C. diff exception
- Standard precautions for all blood/body fluids
- PPE don/doff and no hallway wear of isolation gear
- Contact, droplet, airborne indications
- MRSA colonization vs. infection awareness per facility policy
- Sharps safety—never recap needles; use sharps containers (when tasks within training)
- Reporting exposures (needlestick, splash) to occupational health immediately

##### Outbreak Mindset
When multiple residents develop vomiting, diarrhea, or respiratory symptoms:
- Report cluster early to nurse and infection prevention
- Enforce sick leave for ill staff
- Stock PPE and hand hygiene supplies
- Restrict new admissions or group activities per direction
- Accurate documentation of symptoms onset times aids investigation

##### Antibiotic Resistance
MRSA and multidrug-resistant organisms require contact precautions and careful antibiotic stewardship—CNAs do not prescribe but can prevent spread with hygiene and isolation compliance.

##### Exam Preparation Tips
Questions often combine pathogens with correct precaution type and hand hygiene method. Remember:
- C. diff = contact + soap and water + sporicidal cleaning
- Influenza/COVID policies = droplet + mask + hand hygiene
- TB active pulmonary = airborne + N95 + negative pressure
- Standard precautions always baseline

##### Professional Responsibility
Infection control is not "extra" work—it is foundational CNA practice. Survey deficiencies affect facility licenses; lapses harm vulnerable residents you know by name.

#### KEY POINTS
- Mandatory CE reflects high HAI stakes in California LTC.
- Clusters need early reporting and strict precautions.
- Match hand hygiene method and PPE to organism transmission.
- Exposure incidents require immediate occupational health reporting.
- Final exam tests combined application, not memorized lists alone.

#### KNOWLEDGE CHECK

**Question 1:** Three residents on one hall develop acute vomiting and watery diarrhea within 24 hours. First CNA action?

**Answer:** Implement contact and enteric precautions per policy for symptomatic residents, strict hand hygiene with soap and water, notify nurse and infection prevention immediately about suspected outbreak, and limit movement until instructed.

**Question 2:** You have a small cut on your hand and must provide peri-care. Safe approach?

**Answer:** Cover cut with waterproof bandage, wear gloves, perform hand hygiene before and after, avoid direct contact between your broken skin and body fluids, and report to occupational health if exposure occurs. Consider reassignment if wound cannot be protected.
