# SECTION 2 — TERMS & DEFINITIONS (5 min)
**Tool:** Canva animated flashcards + ElevenLabs (Maria)
**Format:** 8 terms, ~35 sec each.

## ElevenLabs Script — Maria (warm, clear, ESL pace)

**Card 1 — Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD)**
Alcohol use disorder, or AUD. A medical condition in which a person can't control their drinking despite harm to their health, safety, or life. It ranges from mild to severe. It is a treatable illness with biological roots — not a moral failing or weakness. People recover at every age.

**Card 2 — Not a Moral Failing**
Not a moral failing. A crucial idea: alcohol use disorder is an illness, not bad character, weakness, or a choice to be a "bad person." Judgment and shame drive the problem into hiding and away from help. Compassion and treating it as the medical condition it is are what open the door to recovery.

**Card 3 — Hidden / Late-Onset Drinking**
Hidden and late-onset drinking. In older adults, alcohol problems are often invisible — done alone at home, without obvious work or social fallout. Some elders have drunk heavily for decades; others begin drinking heavily late in life, often after loss, grief, retirement, loneliness, or pain. Both are easily missed.

**Card 4 — Increased Sensitivity with Aging**
Increased sensitivity with aging. As the body ages, it processes alcohol less efficiently and holds more of it — so the same drink affects an older person more strongly and for longer. This means even "moderate" drinking can cause real harm in elders, and amounts that were once fine become dangerous.

**Card 5 — Alcohol-Medication Interaction**
Alcohol-medication interaction. When alcohol combines dangerously with medications — sedatives, pain medicines, blood thinners, diabetes and blood-pressure drugs, and many others. These interactions can cause excess sedation, falls, bleeding, dangerous blood sugar or pressure changes, and worse. With the many medications elders take, this is a major danger.

**Card 6 — Alcohol Withdrawal**
Alcohol withdrawal. The symptoms that appear when someone who drinks heavily suddenly stops or sharply cuts back — which can happen when a resident enters a facility. It ranges from tremors, sweating, and anxiety to a life-threatening medical emergency. Withdrawal is recognized and reported immediately — never managed by a CNA.

**Card 7 — Delirium Tremens (DTs)**
Delirium tremens, the DTs. The most severe, life-threatening form of alcohol withdrawal — with severe confusion, agitation, hallucinations, fever, racing heart, and seizures. It can be fatal without urgent medical care. The DTs are a medical emergency; recognizing the warning signs early and escalating fast saves lives.

**Card 8 — Recovery & Non-Judgment**
Recovery and non-judgment. Alcohol use disorder is treatable, and recovery is possible at any age through medical care, counseling, and support. The CNA's part is non-judgmental support — warmth, respect, encouragement, and reporting — never shame, lectures, or policing. Compassion supports recovery; judgment blocks it.

> **Alcohol and Older Adults: Common, Hidden, and More Dangerous Than People Think**
>
> Alcohol use disorder in older adults is more common than most people assume, and it's growing. A meaningful share of older adults drink in ways that harm their health, and the numbers have been rising as the population ages. Yet among all age groups, elders are the most likely to have an alcohol problem go unrecognized — for reasons worth understanding, because they point straight at the CNA's value.
>
> The first reason it hides is that it doesn't look like the stereotype. An older person's drinking often happens alone, at home, without the job loss, arrests, or public disruption that flag it in younger people. The second reason is disguise: alcohol's effects in elders — falls, unsteadiness, confusion, memory problems, poor sleep, self-neglect, worsening health — look exactly like aging, dementia, and other illnesses, so they get attributed to those instead. Screening for alcohol is also done less often in older adults, partly out of an assumption that it isn't an issue at this age. The result is a problem that is both quietly conducted and easily misread.
>
> What makes it especially dangerous is biology and pharmacy. As the body ages, it processes alcohol less efficiently, body water decreases, and the brain becomes more sensitive — so the same amount of alcohol produces higher levels and stronger effects in an older person than it once did. Drinking that was "fine" for decades can become harmful without any increase in amount. On top of this, older adults take many medications, and alcohol interacts dangerously with a long list of them — sedatives and sleep aids, opioid pain medications, blood thinners, diabetes medications, blood pressure drugs, and others — interactions that can cause oversedation, falls, dangerous bleeding, blood sugar and pressure crises, and life-threatening events. Alcohol also directly multiplies the risk of the falls, fractures, and confusion that already threaten elders.
>
> And there's an acute danger specific to care settings: withdrawal. When a person who has been drinking heavily is admitted to a hospital or facility and suddenly has no access to alcohol, they can go into withdrawal — which ranges from uncomfortable to a true medical emergency. Severe withdrawal, including delirium tremens, can be fatal without prompt treatment, and it can begin within a day or two of the last drink. This means a newly admitted resident whose heavy drinking wasn't known can develop a life-threatening emergency on the unit — and early recognition is what saves them.
>
> The hopeful and humane core: alcohol use disorder is treatable, and older adults can and do recover, often responding very well to treatment and support. What helps recovery is the opposite of judgment — compassion, respect, and treating it as the illness it is. The CNA's role flows from all of this: notice the hidden signs that others attribute to aging, recognize the emergency of withdrawal and escalate fast, watch for interactions and injury, and support residents without a shred of shame or lecturing. The bottom line: alcohol problems in elders are common, hidden, and unusually dangerous — and the CNA's watchful, non-judgmental vigilance protects lives.

## ElevenLabs Script — Maria

**Slide 1 — What alcohol use disorder is**
Alcohol use disorder is a medical condition in which a person continues drinking despite it harming their health, relationships, safety, or life — and finds it very hard to stop or control. It involves real changes in the brain that drive craving and dependence. It exists on a spectrum from mild to severe. Understanding it as an illness — like diabetes or heart disease — rather than a moral failing is the foundation for recognizing it and helping.

**Slide 2 — Why "moral failing" thinking is so harmful**
Alcohol problems carry heavy stigma — the idea that the person is weak, bad, or choosing to harm themselves. This thinking is both wrong and damaging. It's wrong because addiction is an illness with biological roots, not a character flaw. And it's damaging because shame and judgment drive the problem into hiding, push people away from help, and make honest conversation impossible. Replacing judgment with compassion isn't softness — it's what actually opens the path to recovery.

**Slide 3 — Why it hides in older adults**
Alcohol problems in elders are especially easy to miss. Older people often drink alone and at home, without the public consequences that reveal it in younger people. And alcohol's effects — falls, confusion, memory problems, unsteadiness, poor self-care, declining health — look just like aging, dementia, or other illnesses, so they get blamed on those. The problem is both conducted quietly and disguised by its own symptoms. A watchful caregiver who knows what to look for is often the one who sees through it.

**Slide 4 — Why aging makes alcohol more dangerous**
A key fact: the same drink affects an older body more. With age, the body processes alcohol less efficiently and holds higher concentrations, and the aging brain is more sensitive to it. So drinking that was once harmless can become harmful with no increase in amount, and "moderate" drinking can cause real damage in an elder. This heightened sensitivity is why alcohol so readily worsens falls, confusion, and overall decline in older adults.

**Slide 5 — The medication danger**
Older adults take many medications, and alcohol interacts dangerously with a long list of them. With sedatives and sleep aids, it deepens sedation dangerously. With opioid pain medications, it can suppress breathing. With blood thinners, it raises bleeding risk. With diabetes medications, it can crash blood sugar. With blood pressure drugs, it can drop pressure dangerously. These interactions can be serious or fatal — which is why alcohol use is never a private side matter in a resident on medications; it's a real safety issue the team needs to know about.

**Slide 6 — Alcohol withdrawal: a medical emergency**
This is the most urgent fact in the course. When a person who drinks heavily suddenly stops — which happens when a resident is admitted and loses access to alcohol — their body, used to alcohol, reacts. Mild withdrawal brings tremors, sweating, anxiety, nausea, and a racing heart. But it can escalate to severe withdrawal and delirium tremens — severe confusion, hallucinations, fever, and seizures — which can be fatal without urgent treatment. Withdrawal can start within a day or two of the last drink. Recognizing it early and reporting immediately is lifesaving. A CNA never manages withdrawal — a CNA recognizes and escalates it.

**Slide 7 — Recovery is real, and judgment blocks it**
End on the hopeful truth: alcohol use disorder is treatable, and older adults recover — often responding very well to treatment, counseling, and support. What helps is compassion, respect, and treating it as the illness it is; what blocks recovery is shame, lectures, and policing. Your role is to support without judgment, recognize the dangers, and report — never to shame or to act as the alcohol police. Hope here is realistic, and your warmth is part of what makes it possible.

With alcohol problems, you watch for the hidden signs, the effects, and — most urgently — the signs of withdrawal. Let's learn them, ending with the emergency.

**[Clip 1 — the hidden signs of an alcohol problem]**
Because elder drinking hides, watch for the quieter signs and patterns. Smelling alcohol on the breath or in the room, or finding bottles. Unexplained or frequent falls, unsteadiness, and injuries. Confusion, memory problems, or mood changes that come and go. Poor self-care, neglected hygiene, and decline. Poor nutrition and missed meals. Sleep problems. **[Clip 2 — signs of intoxication and interactions]**
Watch for signs a resident is currently affected by alcohol — or by alcohol mixing with medications: slurred speech, unsteadiness, poor coordination, and falls. Confusion or drowsiness beyond their normal. **[Clip 3 — the early signs of withdrawal]**

Agitation and hallucinations — seeing or hearing things that aren't there. High fever. A racing heart. Profuse sweating. And seizures. A resident showing these — especially severe confusion, hallucinations, or a seizure, in someone who may have been drinking heavily — is a medical emergency. > **Why Alcohol Problems Develop in Older Adults — and Why It's an Illness, Not a Failing**
>
> Understanding what drives alcohol use disorder does two important things: it replaces judgment with compassion, and it helps caregivers see the whole person. Addiction is never simply a choice to be a bad person — it arises from a mix of biology, life circumstances, and the powerful way alcohol acts on the brain.
>
> On the biological side, alcohol use disorder involves real changes in brain chemistry. Repeated heavy drinking alters the brain's reward and stress systems, creating craving and dependence that are genuinely hard to overcome by willpower alone — which is part of why it's classified as a medical illness. There's a genetic component too; it can run in families. This is the core reason shame and "just stop" thinking fail: the condition isn't located in weak character, it's located in a changed brain and body.
>
> For older adults specifically, there are two common paths. Some have lived with alcohol use disorder for decades, carrying a long-standing condition into old age. Others develop a drinking problem late in life, sometimes for the first time — and the triggers for late-onset drinking are deeply human and deserve compassion. They include the accumulated losses of aging: the death of a spouse and friends; retirement and the loss of work, routine, and identity; loneliness and isolation; boredom and lack of purpose; chronic pain and illness; depression and anxiety, which often travel with alcohol use, each worsening the other; and the sheer emptiness and grief that late life can bring. Many late-life drinkers are, at bottom, people in pain who turned to alcohol to cope — which is sad, not shameful.
>
> Then there's alcohol's own role. It provides short-term relief from loneliness, grief, pain, anxiety, and sleeplessness — and that very relief drives continued use, even as it worsens the underlying problems and the person's health. The pattern feeds itself: drinking to cope with pain creates more pain to cope with.
>
> Here's what it means for the CNA, and it's both humbling and empowering. You can't treat the addiction — that's for professionals — and you can't undo a resident's losses. But you can address some of what drives it: the loneliness, isolation, and lack of purpose that you learned about in depression and anxiety care are often the same forces feeding late-life drinking. Genuine connection, engagement, purpose, and support work on real contributors. And critically, your non-judgmental stance — seeing the person and their pain rather than a character flaw — is what makes it possible for a resident to be honest, accept help, and recover. Compassion isn't just kindness here; it's a working part of care.
>
> The takeaway: alcohol use disorder comes from biology, life pain, and alcohol's grip — not from weakness — and while professionals treat the addiction, the CNA supports the human being underneath it with connection, vigilance, and complete absence of judgment.

Alcohol use disorder is treated by medical and addiction professionals — and the CNA's job is recognition, vigilance, non-judgmental support, and reporting, with one piece that's truly lifesaving. **Recognizing and escalating withdrawal — the lifesaving piece.** This is the most critical thing in the course. **Safety, nutrition, and the whole person.** Support fall prevention for a resident affected by alcohol, support nutrition and hydration often neglected with heavy drinking, and provide patient, dignified care throughout.

The scope point for this section: you never diagnose, counsel, manage withdrawal, medicate, or police drinking — but you recognize and urgently escalate withdrawal, watch for interactions and injury, support recovery without judgment, and report. Recognizing the withdrawal emergency and supporting without shame are the care you deliver.

# SECTION 8 — PREVENTION (5 min)
**Tool:** Canva kinetic typography + Gemini Omni B-roll + ElevenLabs (Maria)
**Format:** 7 tips, text animates as Maria reads, B-roll behind. Focus: protecting residents from alcohol's dangers and supporting recovery.

## ElevenLabs Script — Maria

With alcohol problems, prevention means protecting residents from harm and supporting their recovery. Here are seven ways.

**One. Notice the hidden signs — and report them.** Because elder drinking hides and disguises itself as aging, your watchful noticing is the first protection. Report patterns of falls, unsteadiness, confusion, decline, neglect, or the smell of alcohol — without judgment — so the problem can be recognized and addressed. What's seen can be helped; what stays hidden keeps harming.

**Two. Watch for and report withdrawal — fast.** For any resident, especially a newly admitted one, watch for early withdrawal signs — tremors, sweating, anxiety, racing heart — and report them immediately. Catching withdrawal early lets it be treated before it turns dangerous. This is prevention of a medical emergency, and it's one of the most important things you do.

**Three. Guard against dangerous interactions.** Know that alcohol mixes dangerously with elders' medications, and watch for excessive sedation and interaction signs, especially in residents on sedatives, sleep aids, or pain medicines. Report these promptly. Your vigilance prevents an interaction from becoming an emergency.

**Four. Prevent falls and injury.** A resident affected by alcohol is at high risk of falls and injury. Support fall-prevention measures, watch unsteady residents closely, keep their environment safe, and report unsteadiness and falls. Protecting them from injury is concrete, daily prevention.

**Five. Support nutrition, hydration, and health.** Heavy drinking often comes with poor nutrition and neglected health. Encourage meals and fluids, support self-care, and report poor intake and decline. Supporting the body that alcohol depletes helps protect the resident's overall health.

**Six. Offer connection and purpose.** Because loneliness, grief, and lack of purpose often drive late-life drinking, the connection and engagement you provide work on real causes. Offer genuine warmth and companionship, counter isolation, support meaningful activity and purpose — and watch for the depression and anxiety that travel with alcohol use. Connection is prevention that reaches the root.

**Seven. Support recovery with zero judgment.** The strongest thing you bring to recovery is an absence of shame. ## CMS case_study Block Content

**Title:** Two Days After Admission

**Case narrative:**
Mr. Brennan, 72, had been admitted to the facility two days earlier after a fall at home left him unable to manage alone. He was a quiet, private man; his family, in passing, had mentioned he "liked his evening drinks." No one had thought much of it. His CNA, this shift, was Imelda.

**The change:** On Mr. Brennan's second full day, Imelda noticed him changing through the morning, and it worried her. His hands had developed a visible tremor. He was sweating heavily though the room was cool. He seemed anxious and agitated, unable to settle, his heart visibly racing. And as the hours passed he grew confused — and then said, looking at the corner of the room, that there were people standing there who weren't.

**The easy wrong answer:** A coworker offered the comfortable explanation: "New place, new bed — he's just anxious and disoriented from the move. Lots of them are the first few days. Give him time to settle in." On the surface it was plausible — new admissions are often unsettled. But Imelda's training surfaced a different, more urgent pattern. A resident admitted two days ago, family says he drank regularly at home, now suddenly cut off from alcohol — and showing tremors, sweating, agitation, a racing heart, confusion, and now hallucinations. That wasn't adjustment anxiety. That was the picture of alcohol withdrawal, and the hallucinations told her it was becoming severe.

**What Imelda knew:** She knew the crucial facts: that when a heavy drinker suddenly stops — exactly what happens on admission — withdrawal can set in within a day or two; that it can escalate to delirium tremens, with severe confusion, hallucinations, fever, and seizures; and that the DTs can be fatal without urgent treatment. She also knew precisely what was and wasn't her job. It was not her job to manage it, to find him a drink, or to wait it out. It was her job to recognize it and escalate it immediately.

**What Imelda did:** She reported it to the nurse right away, with the full, specific picture: "Mr. Brennan was admitted two days ago, his family said he drank regularly at home, and this morning he's developed hand tremors, heavy sweating, agitation, a racing heart, he's getting confused, and now he's seeing things that aren't there. I'm worried this is alcohol withdrawal." She didn't diagnose it as a doctor would, didn't try to treat it, didn't go looking for alcohol to calm him, and didn't leave him alone — she stayed with him, kept him safe and calm, while help came.

**The response:** Because Imelda recognized the pattern and escalated immediately with the key detail — the timing and the family's note about his drinking — Mr. Brennan was assessed urgently and started on proper medical treatment for alcohol withdrawal before it progressed to full delirium tremens. He was monitored closely and treated through the withdrawal safely. The nurse was direct with Imelda afterward: "If that had been written off as 'new-place jitters' for another day, this could have gone to full DTs and a seizure — and people die from that. You caught it because you knew the timing, you knew what those signs meant together, and you told us the drinking history. That's a save."

**The other half:** As Mr. Brennan recovered and his situation came into the open, Imelda was struck by something: he was deeply ashamed. He apologized to her, called himself weak, seemed to expect disgust. She gave him none — only the same warmth and respect she gave anyone, treating his condition as the illness it was, never lecturing or judging. That non-judgment, she understood, was its own kind of care: it was part of what would let him accept help going forward instead of hiding in shame.

**The outcome:** Mr. Brennan came safely through a withdrawal that could have killed him, and met, in his shame, a caregiver who treated him as a worthy human being rather than a failure. The save was clinical and human at once. The nurse summed it up: "You recognized a medical emergency that hides as 'just settling in,' and you treated the man with dignity through the worst of it. Both of those are exactly the job."

Recognize the emergency, escalate fast, judge no one — that is caring for a resident with an alcohol problem.
