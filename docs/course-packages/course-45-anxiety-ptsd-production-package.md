# SECTION 2 — TERMS & DEFINITIONS (5 min)
**Tool:** Canva animated flashcards + ElevenLabs (Maria)
**Format:** 8 terms, ~35 sec each.

## ElevenLabs Script — Maria (warm, clear, ESL pace)

**Card 1 — Anxiety Disorder**
Anxiety disorder. Persistent, excessive fear or worry that doesn't fade, is out of proportion to the situation, and interferes with daily life. More than ordinary worry, it's a real, treatable medical condition — common in older adults and often overlooked or mistaken for physical illness.

**Card 2 — Panic Attack**
Panic attack. A sudden wave of intense fear that peaks within minutes — with a pounding heart, chest tightness, breathlessness, trembling, sweating, dizziness, and a feeling of doom or losing control. It can look like a heart attack, which is why new or severe symptoms are always reported. Panic attacks are terrifying but not dangerous in themselves.

**Card 3 — PTSD**
PTSD. Post-traumatic stress disorder. A condition that can develop after experiencing or witnessing something terrifying — combat, assault, abuse, disaster. The trauma intrudes into the present through nightmares, flashbacks, constant alertness, and intense reactions to reminders. It can persist for decades, and it's treatable.

**Card 4 — Trigger**
Trigger. Anything that reminds a trauma survivor of their trauma and sets off intense fear or a trauma response — a sound, smell, touch, situation, or feeling of being trapped or powerless. Triggers in care settings include being undressed, confined, touched without warning, or loud sudden noises. Knowing a resident's triggers helps you avoid them.

**Card 5 — Flashback**
Flashback. When a trauma survivor doesn't just remember the trauma — they re-experience it, feeling as if it's happening right now. During a flashback, a resident may not know where they are, may see you as a threat, and may act to protect themselves. Responding calmly, without grabbing or arguing, is essential.

**Card 6 — Hypervigilance**
Hypervigilance. HY-per-VIJ-ih-lance. Being constantly on guard — scanning for danger, startling easily, unable to relax, sleeping poorly. A common PTSD sign. A hypervigilant resident may sit facing the door, react strongly to sudden approaches, and need extra predictability and warning before care.

**Card 7 — Trauma-Informed Care**
Trauma-informed care. Care given on the assumption that any resident may carry trauma — protecting them through predictability, offering choices and control, asking consent, explaining before touching, and avoiding known triggers. It costs nothing, helps everyone, and prevents care itself from becoming frightening.

**Card 8 — Grounding**
Grounding. Gentle techniques that help an anxious or triggered person return to the present — a calm voice, saying their name, reminding them where they are and that they're safe, focusing on the here and now. CNAs use simple grounding by staying calm, present, and reassuring — never by arguing or restraining.

> **Anxiety and Old Trauma: The Invisible Weight Many Residents Carry**
>
> Anxiety is among the most common mental health conditions in older adults — by many estimates affecting a meaningful share of elders, and more in long-term care — yet it's frequently overlooked, partly because anxious elders often describe physical symptoms rather than worry, and partly because, like depression, it gets dismissed as just part of aging or illness. Untreated anxiety in elders is linked to worse physical health, more disability, poorer sleep, more falls, and lower quality of life. It is real, burdensome, and treatable.
>
> PTSD deserves special attention in this population, for a reason that becomes obvious once stated: today's older adults have lived through an enormous amount. Among them are combat veterans of past wars; survivors of childhood and domestic abuse; refugees and immigrants who fled violence and upheaval; survivors of assaults, accidents, disasters, and devastating losses. Studies of older adults, and especially of veterans and long-term care populations, find that significant numbers carry trauma histories — many having never spoken of them, having coped silently for fifty or sixty years. Trauma this old does not necessarily fade; it can persist, resurface under stress, or emerge for the first time in late life.
>
> Long-term care itself, unintentionally, can press on these old wounds. Consider what care involves from a trauma survivor's perspective: losing control over one's body, schedule, and space; being undressed and bathed by strangers; being touched without warning; confinement; loud and sudden noises; institutional settings that may echo past institutions. For an abuse survivor, intimate care by a stranger can feel like the past returning. For a combat veteran, a sudden noise or being startled awake can land like incoming fire. Research on trauma in care settings shows these everyday features of care can trigger fear, panic, and "behaviors" — resistance, aggression, crying out — that are often misread as a resident being difficult, when they are in fact a frightened person protecting themselves.
>
> Dementia adds a profound twist: as recent memory fails, old memories — including traumatic ones — can become vivid and present. A veteran with dementia may genuinely be back in the war; an abuse survivor may genuinely believe the danger is now. This resurfacing of trauma in dementia is well documented and explains some of the fear and agitation seen in memory care.
>
> The hopeful core is this: trauma-informed care — predictability, explaining before touching, offering choice and consent, avoiding known triggers, calm and respectful approach — measurably reduces fear, distress, and difficult moments in care, and it requires no equipment and no extra cost, only knowledge and habit. CNAs deliver most hands-on care, so CNAs deliver most trauma-informed care. The bottom line: many residents carry invisible anxiety and old trauma; ordinary care can unknowingly trigger it; and the CNA's trauma-informed approach can turn care from frightening into safe.

# SECTION 4 — FUNDAMENTALS (5 min)
**Tool:** Canva slides + ElevenLabs (Maria)
**Format:** 7 slides, one concept each.

## ElevenLabs Script — Maria

**Slide 1 — Anxiety: when worry becomes illness**
Everyone worries. Anxiety becomes a disorder when the fear and worry are persistent, excessive, out of proportion, and interfering with life — when a person can't turn it off. In the body, anxiety runs the alarm system too hot: racing heart, tense muscles, churning stomach, poor sleep, restlessness. In older adults it often shows up as physical complaints, health worries, fear of falling, or clinging to routines — more than as stated "worry." It is a real condition, and it's treatable.

**Slide 2 — The panic attack**
A panic attack is the alarm system firing at full blast for no immediate reason: sudden, intense fear that peaks in minutes, with a pounding heart, chest tightness, trouble breathing, trembling, sweating, dizziness, and a terrifying sense of doom or losing control. Two things to hold together: a panic attack is not in itself dangerous — it passes — but its symptoms overlap with heart attacks and other emergencies, so new or severe symptoms are always reported. Never assume; let the nurse sort out which it is.

**Slide 3 — PTSD: when trauma won't stay in the past**
PTSD develops after terror — combat, assault, abuse, disaster. The mind, overwhelmed, fails to file the trauma away as "past," so it keeps intruding into the present: nightmares; flashbacks where the person re-experiences the event as if now; constant alertness, called hypervigilance; avoiding reminders; being easily startled; and intense distress at triggers. PTSD can begin soon after trauma or surface decades later, and many elders have carried it silently their whole adult lives. It is not weakness — it is an injury, and it's treatable.

**Slide 4 — Triggers: how the past gets touched**
A trigger is anything that reminds the survivor's nervous system of the trauma — and the reminder doesn't need to be logical or obvious. Sounds, smells, touches, positions, confinement, darkness, being startled, feeling powerless. And here's the hard truth about care settings: daily care is full of potential triggers — being undressed, bathed, touched without warning, having someone stand over you, loud machinery, locked doors. When a resident panics, resists, or "fights" during care, the question to carry is: what just got touched? Often it's not the task — it's the trauma underneath it.

**Slide 5 — The flashback, from the inside**
Understand what a flashback is like from inside: the person is not remembering the trauma — they are IN it. A veteran may be back in combat; an abuse survivor may be back in danger. In that state, they may not recognize where they are, and they may see you — reaching toward them, standing over them — as the threat. Their resistance isn't aggression at you; it's self-protection inside a reality you can't see. This understanding changes everything about how you respond: calmly, without grabbing, without arguing about what's real.

**Slide 6 — Dementia and resurfacing trauma**
Dementia adds a profound layer. As dementia erodes recent memory, older memories grow relatively stronger — and that includes traumatic ones. A resident who coped for sixty years may, with dementia, find the war or the abuse vividly present again, without the defenses they once had. Night fears, terror during bathing, calling out to people from the past, "agitation" with no visible cause — in a resident with dementia, these may be old trauma resurfacing. Knowing a resident's history, where it's shared with the team, helps everyone understand and protect them.

**Slide 7 — Trauma-informed care: the answer you deliver**
Here's the practice that answers all of it: trauma-informed care. Its premise is humble — you can't know who carries trauma, so you care for everyone as if they might. Its habits are simple: be predictable — same routines, no surprises. Explain before you do — every step, before every touch. Ask consent — "Is it okay if I...?" Offer choices and control wherever possible. Approach from the front, calmly, at eye level. Avoid known triggers. These habits cost nothing, comfort every resident — and for a trauma survivor, they're the difference between care and terror.

## ElevenLabs Script — James

Anxiety and trauma show themselves in the body, in behavior, and in moments of care. Let's learn to recognize them — and what to report.

**[Clip 1 — the signs of anxiety in elders]**
Anxiety in older adults often hides behind the physical. Watch for: persistent worry, or repeated fearful questions and reassurance-seeking. Restlessness, tension, trembling, or being unable to settle. Physical complaints — racing heart, stomach trouble, dizziness, aches — without clear cause. Poor sleep. Avoiding activities, or fear of falling that goes beyond caution. Clinging to routines and distress when they change. Irritability. A resident who seems "wound tight," checks and rechecks, or can't be reassured may be living with real anxiety — and it's reportable, because it's treatable.

**[Clip 2 — recognizing a panic attack]**
A panic attack is sudden and intense: overwhelming fear peaking within minutes, a pounding or racing heart, chest tightness or pain, shortness of breath, trembling, sweating, dizziness, numbness or tingling, and a feeling of doom, dying, or losing control. The resident may genuinely believe they're dying. Two responses at once: stay calm and present — your steady calm is the treatment you deliver — and report it, because these symptoms overlap with heart attack and other emergencies, and the nurse must sort out which this is. New chest pain or breathing trouble is never written off as "just panic."

**[Clip 3 — PTSD signs and trigger responses]**
PTSD shows itself in patterns and in moments. The patterns: hypervigilance — always on guard, startling easily, sitting facing the door, sleeping poorly. Nightmares and disturbed nights. Avoiding certain situations, places, or kinds of care. Emotional numbness or detachment. And the moments — trigger responses during care: sudden fear, panic, or "fighting" during bathing, dressing, or personal care. Terror at being approached from behind, startled, or confined. A resident suddenly somewhere else — eyes far away, speaking to people who aren't there, reliving something. These aren't "behaviors" to manage; they're fear to understand. Note what happened just before — the trigger — because that pattern protects them next time.

**[Clip 4 — what to report]**
Report it all, specifically. Report persistent anxiety, panic attacks, and any new or severe physical symptoms urgently. Report trigger responses with the gold detail: what was happening just before — the time of day, the task, the approach, the sound — because identifying triggers lets the whole team adjust care. Report nightmares, night terrors, and fears the resident expresses. And remember from your depression training: anxiety and trauma travel with depression, so watch for hopelessness and any talk of not wanting to live — and respond exactly as you learned: take it seriously, stay, report immediately, never promise secrecy.

**[Summary — split screen]**
Four pictures. Anxiety: persistent worry, restlessness, physical complaints, poor sleep, reassurance-seeking. Panic attack: sudden intense fear, pounding heart, breathlessness, doom — stay calm AND report; never assume it's "just panic." PTSD: hypervigilance, nightmares, avoidance — and trigger responses during care, where resistance is often fear. Report: the signs, the symptoms, and especially what happened just before a trigger response.

The rule underneath it all: recognize anxiety behind the physical complaints, treat panic symptoms as reportable, read "difficult behavior" during care as possible fear — and always note what came just before.

> **Where Anxiety and Old Trauma Come From — and What Care Can Touch**
>
> Anxiety and PTSD in older adults have understandable causes, and seeing them clearly does two things: it replaces judgment with compassion, and it shows exactly where a caregiver's choices matter.
>
> Anxiety in elders typically comes from several sources stacking. There's biology — anxiety runs in families and involves the brain's alarm system. There are medical contributors: thyroid problems, heart and lung conditions, pain, and certain medications can all produce or worsen anxiety, which is one reason new anxiety deserves medical attention rather than dismissal. And there are the realities of late life: health fears, fear of falling, loss of independence and control, grief, financial worry, and the uncertainty that comes with declining abilities. A care facility can ease some of these — and can worsen others, particularly the loss of control over one's own day, body, and space. Anxiety also frequently travels with depression, each feeding the other.
>
> PTSD's cause is, by definition, trauma — and the generation now in long-term care has lived through much: combat in past wars; childhood and domestic abuse in eras when no one spoke of it; sexual assault; displacement, persecution, and flight; catastrophic accidents and losses. Many survivors coped by silence and work and routine for decades. What brings old trauma forward in late life? Several things: the losses and stresses of aging strain the defenses that held it down; retirement and quiet leave more room for memories; declining health creates helplessness that echoes the original trauma; and dementia, as it erodes recent memory, can make decades-old traumatic memories vivid and present again. So trauma that "was handled" for fifty years can resurface exactly when a person enters care.
>
> Then there's the part a CNA most directly touches: what makes care settings triggering. The common threads of trauma are powerlessness, violation, confinement, and threat — and ordinary care can unintentionally echo all of them. Being undressed and bathed by a stranger. Being touched without warning or consent. Someone standing over you, approaching from behind, grabbing your arm to help. Locked units, alarms, shouting, sudden noises, being startled awake. Loss of choice over when and how things happen. None of this is anyone's fault — but each is a cause of fear that caregiving habits can remove.
>
> And that's the empowering takeaway. You can't change a resident's biology, history, or losses. But the triggers built into how care is delivered — surprise, powerlessness, unexplained touch, lost choice — are precisely what trauma-informed care eliminates: predictability, explaining first, asking consent, offering control, calm frontal approach, knowing and avoiding individual triggers. The CNA can't heal the trauma — that's for professionals — but the CNA controls whether daily care presses on the wound or protects it.

# SECTION 7 — MEDICATIONS & TREATMENT (5 min)
**Tool:** Canva infographic + ElevenLabs (James)
**Format:** Color-coded board. GREEN = the CNA's trauma-informed practice + calm response. RED = report/never. Centerpiece: trauma-informed care habits + the in-the-moment response to panic/flashback.

## ElevenLabs Script — James

Anxiety and PTSD are treated by professionals — therapy and sometimes medication — but how daily care is delivered is itself part of the treatment, and that's the CNA's. Let's walk through it, starting with the rule that never bends. A CNA does not diagnose anxiety or PTSD, does not provide therapy or counseling, does not give or adjust medications, and does not ask a resident to recount or process their trauma — opening trauma is a professional's work; your work is making the present safe.

**Trauma-informed care — your daily treatment.** These habits, with every resident, every time. Be predictable: consistent routines, consistent caregivers where possible, no surprises. Explain before you do: every step, in simple words, before every touch — "I'm going to help you with your sleeve now, alright?" Ask consent and wait for it. Offer choices and control wherever possible — when, how, what order, who. Approach from the front, calmly, at eye level, never startling or grabbing. Knock, announce yourself, keep the resident covered and dignified during personal care. And learn each resident's known triggers from the care plan and avoid them. These habits protect every resident, and for trauma survivors they make care safe instead of terrifying.

**Responding to a panic attack — calm is the treatment.** When a resident panics: stay with them and stay visibly calm — your steadiness is what their alarm system borrows. Speak slowly, simply, and reassuringly: "I'm here with you. You're safe. This will pass." Don't dismiss the fear or tell them to "calm down." Encourage slow breathing by doing it with them. Reduce stimulation — quiet, space, fewer people. And report it — always — because panic symptoms overlap with cardiac and other emergencies, and the nurse decides which this is.

**Responding to a flashback or trigger response — safety without force.** When a resident is triggered or in a flashback: stay calm and give space — do not grab, restrain, corner, or argue about what's real. Speak gently and identify yourself and the present: their name, your name, where they are, that they're safe — simple grounding. Remove or stop the trigger if you can: pause the task, lower the noise, step back. Approach from the front, hands visible, no sudden moves. Let it pass without forcing the task — care can wait; safety and trust can't be un-broken. Then report what happened and what came just before, so the team can adjust.

**Supporting professional treatment.** Support therapy participation and the care plan. Support medications as the nurse administers them, report side effects and refusals — and note that some anxiety medications can cause drowsiness, unsteadiness, and fall risk in elders, so watch and report those. Report patterns: what triggers, what calms, what's working.

**Comfort, routine, and connection.** Deliver the daily stabilizers: consistent routines, calm environment, adequate sleep support, gentle activity, and warm connection — the same connection you learned in depression care, because these conditions travel together.

The scope point for this section: you never diagnose, counsel, medicate, or open trauma — but you deliver trauma-informed care that prevents triggering, respond to panic and flashbacks with calm and grounding, support treatment, and report patterns. The way you give care is the treatment you own.

# SECTION 8 — PREVENTION (5 min)
**Tool:** Canva kinetic typography + Gemini Omni B-roll + ElevenLabs (Maria)
**Format:** 7 tips, text animates as Maria reads, B-roll behind. Focus: preventing fear and triggering through how care is delivered.

## ElevenLabs Script — Maria

With anxiety and trauma, prevention means delivering care that never becomes frightening. Here are seven ways.

**One. Make care predictable.** Keep routines consistent — same order, same times, same approach, and consistent caregivers where possible. Tell residents what's coming next, today and tomorrow. Predictability is safety to an anxious mind and a trauma survivor: when nothing is a surprise, nothing is an ambush.

**Two. Explain before every touch.** Narrate care in simple, calm words before you do each step — "I'm going to lift your arm now." Never touch without warning, never start a task unannounced, never approach from behind or startle. Unexplained touch is one of care's most common triggers, and you remove it with a sentence.

**Three. Ask consent and offer choices.** Ask permission — "Is it okay if we do your bath now?" — and wait for the answer. Offer real choices: when, in what order, shower or bed bath, which clothes. Trauma is powerlessness; choice and consent return power. Every choice you offer is prevention.

**Four. Protect dignity and privacy fiercely.** Knock and announce yourself. Keep residents covered during personal care, exposing only what's needed. Be matter-of-fact and respectful with intimate care. For abuse survivors especially, dignified, consented, unhurried personal care is the difference between care and re-living harm.

**Five. Keep the environment calm.** Reduce sudden loud noises, alarms, shouting, and chaos where you can. Approach calmly, move unhurriedly, keep your voice low and warm. Don't startle residents awake — wake them gently. A calm environment keeps every alarm system, including a hypervigilant one, at rest.

**Six. Learn and avoid each resident's triggers.** Read the care plan for known triggers and honor them — the veteran who can't be approached from behind, the resident who panics with the door closed, the survivor who needs a same-gender caregiver for bathing where possible. And when you discover a new trigger, report it so the whole team can avoid it. Known triggers, avoided, are crises prevented.

**Seven. Build trust through reliability and warmth.** Be the caregiver who always explains, always asks, never surprises, never forces — and who offers steady, genuine warmth. Trust built over weeks is what lets an anxious or traumatized resident relax into care. Your reliability is, quietly, the strongest prevention there is.

## ElevenLabs Intro Audio Script — James (~55 sec)

With anxiety and trauma, the judgments that matter most are responding correctly to a resident in a flashback, and never assuming dangerous symptoms are "just anxiety." You're about to work through two situations. Read each carefully, choose your action, and you'll get feedback right away. Keep the core ideas in mind: in a flashback, you give space, ground gently, and never grab or argue — and chest pain with breathlessness is reported, every time, no matter the resident's anxiety history.

## CMS scenario_question Block 1 — THE FLASHBACK DURING CARE (space, grounding, no force)

**Scenario text:**
Mr. Kowalczyk, a combat veteran with dementia, suddenly becomes terrified during his morning care when a food cart crashes loudly in the hallway. His eyes go distant, he shouts words you don't understand, takes a defensive crouch in the corner, and when you step toward him he raises his fists — he doesn't seem to recognize you or know where he is. A coworker says, "Grab his arms before he hurts himself — and tell him there's no war, he's being ridiculous." What SHOULD the CNA do?

**Options:**
- A. Grab his arms to restrain him and tell him firmly there's no war
- B. Step back and give space, stay calm with hands visible, gently ground him — his name, your name, "you're safe, you're at [facility]" — pause the task, and report it ✅ **CORRECT**
- C. Keep doing his care quickly to get it over with
- D. Argue with him until he admits where he is

**Feedback (correct):** Exactly right. The crash triggered a flashback — and with dementia eroding recent memory, Mr. Kowalczyk may genuinely be back in combat, seeing you as a threat. Grabbing him would confirm the threat and could injure you both; arguing about what's real or calling him ridiculous fights his reality and deepens the terror. Instead: step back and give him space — don't corner him. Stay visibly calm, hands open and visible, no sudden moves, approaching only from the front. Ground him gently and simply: say his name, identify yourself, tell him where he is and that he's safe — calm, repeated, unhurried. Reduce the trigger: quiet the environment. Pause the task — care can wait. When it passes, reassure and comfort him. Then report the whole event and the trigger — the loud crash — so the team can protect him from it next time. Space, calm, grounding, no force: that's exactly how a flashback is met.

**Feedback (incorrect):** Mr. Kowalczyk is in a flashback — possibly genuinely back in combat in his mind — and sees threat, not help. Grabbing, restraining, rushing the task, or arguing about what's real deepens the terror and invites injury. The right response: step back, give space, stay calm with hands visible, ground him gently (his name, your name, "you're safe, you're here"), pause the task, and report the trigger.

## CMS scenario_question Block 2 — "JUST ANXIETY"? (never assume — report the symptoms)

**Scenario text:**
Mrs. Abellard has a known anxiety disorder and has had panic attacks before. This afternoon she's suddenly frightened, with chest tightness, a racing heart, and shortness of breath. A coworker waves it off: "She does this — it's just one of her panic attacks. Sit with her and don't bother the nurse." Something feels different to you — she looks grayer than usual and the symptoms aren't easing. What SHOULD the CNA do?

**Options:**
- A. Assume it's just a panic attack like always and don't tell the nurse
- B. Stay with her, keep her calm — AND report the chest tightness, racing heart, and breathlessness to the nurse promptly, because these symptoms overlap with cardiac emergencies ✅ **CORRECT**
- C. Tell her to calm down and breathe, and leave her to ride it out alone
- D. Have her walk it off

**Feedback (correct):** Right. This is one of the most important judgments in anxiety care: panic attack symptoms — chest tightness, racing heart, breathlessness, sense of doom — overlap heavily with heart attack and other emergencies, and a resident with an anxiety history can still have a cardiac event. "She does this" is exactly the assumption that gets a heart attack missed in an anxious resident. So you do both things at once: stay with her and provide the calm, steady presence that helps either way — and report the symptoms to the nurse promptly so they can assess which this is; that's the nurse's call, never yours or a coworker's. Your instinct that something looks different — the gray color, symptoms not easing — is exactly the kind of observation to include. Never leave her alone with these symptoms, and never write off chest symptoms as "just anxiety." Calm support plus prompt reporting covers her either way.

**Feedback (incorrect):** Chest tightness, racing heart, and breathlessness overlap with cardiac emergencies — and an anxiety history doesn't protect her from a heart attack. "It's just her panic attacks" is how cardiac events get missed in anxious residents. The right action: stay with her and keep her calm, AND report the symptoms promptly so the nurse can assess — never assume, and never leave her alone.

# SECTION 11 — BRIEF CASE STUDY (5 min)
**Tool:** CMS case_study block + NotebookLM podcast (FREE)

## CMS case_study Block Content

**Title:** The Bath That Stopped Being a Battle

**Case narrative:**
Mrs. Abellard, 81, had a reputation on the unit before her CNA, Tomas, ever met her: "difficult with care." Bath times were battles — she would tense, cry, beg staff to stop, sometimes strike out. Some staff dreaded being assigned to her; care notes described "resistance to bathing" and "combative behavior." She was also anxious in general: wound tight, startling easily, sleeping poorly, asking the same worried questions again and again.

**What Tomas wondered:** Tomas had just completed training on trauma and care, and one idea had lodged in him: when a resident "fights" care, ask what's being touched underneath the task. Mrs. Abellard's pattern — terror specifically around being undressed and bathed, hypervigilance, the startling, the poor sleep — fit what he'd learned about trauma survivors, for whom intimate care by strangers can feel like harm returning. He didn't need to know her history, and he never asked her to share it — that wasn't his place. He just changed how he gave care, on the assumption that it might be true.

**What Tomas changed:** He rebuilt her bath around trauma-informed habits. He kept it utterly predictable — same time, same routine, told her the day before and the morning of. He asked consent at the start — "Would it be alright if we do your bath now? We'll go at your pace" — and waited for her yes. He explained every single step before doing it — "I'm going to help with your sleeve now, okay?" — and never touched without warning. He gave her every possible choice: the order of things, the water temperature, holding the washcloth to do what she could herself. He kept her covered except the part being washed, draping her with towels, guarding her dignity fiercely. He never rushed, never stood over her, approached from the front, and stopped the moment she seemed distressed — "We can pause. You're in charge here."

**The turn:** It didn't change in a day. But over weeks, the battles faded. Given warning, consent, choices, and cover, Mrs. Abellard's terror had less and less to grip. She began doing parts of her own washing, holding the cloth like something reclaimed. Her tension eased beyond bath time too — she startled less around Tomas, slept better, asked fewer frantic questions. One morning she told him quietly, "You're the first one who ever asked." He reported what worked — the warnings, the choices, the draping, the pacing — and the team adopted her routine, writing it into her care plan so every caregiver protected her the same way.

**The other catch:** Tomas's calm also proved itself in a different moment. One afternoon Mrs. Abellard had sudden chest tightness, a racing heart, and breathlessness — and a coworker shrugged, "That's her anxiety, she does this." Tomas stayed with her, kept her calm — and reported it promptly anyway, because he'd learned panic symptoms overlap with cardiac emergencies and that call belongs to the nurse. The nurse assessed her; that day it was panic, and she was treated and supported. "But you did exactly right," the nurse told him. "The day it isn't panic, that report is what saves her."

**The outcome:** Bath time stopped being a battle because it stopped feeling like an attack. Mrs. Abellard's "combative behavior" had never been combativeness — it had been fear, and when the fear was answered with predictability, consent, choice, and dignity, it subsided. The nurse summed it up: "We spent a year managing her 'behavior.' Tomas spent three weeks removing the reasons for it. Nothing about her changed — everything about how we approached her did."

**Discussion:** Tomas's case is trauma-informed care proven in practice. He read "difficult behavior" as possible fear and asked what care was touching underneath. He never probed her history or asked her to explain — he simply made care safe: predictable, explained, consented, choiceful, dignified, unhurried. He reported the pattern of what worked so the whole team could protect her. And he refused the "it's just her anxiety" trap, reporting cardiac-overlapping symptoms for the nurse to judge. He never diagnosed or counseled; he changed the delivery of care — the part that was always his — and a battle became a bath.

**Reflection Question 1:** Mrs. Abellard was labeled "combative with care." What did Tomas understand her resistance to really be, and which trauma-informed habits dissolved it?

**Reflection Question 2:** When she had chest tightness and breathlessness, a coworker said "that's just her anxiety." Why did Tomas report it anyway, and why is that judgment so important?
