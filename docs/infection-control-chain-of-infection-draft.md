## Readable prose (review copy)

### Chain of Infection

Infection does not happen by chance. For a resident to develop an infection, several conditions must line up in a specific order. Infection prevention experts describe this as the **chain of infection**—six linked steps that must all be present for disease to spread from one person to another (or from the environment to a person).

As a CNA in long-term care or skilled nursing, you are on the front line of breaking that chain every shift. You provide direct care, handle equipment, assist with meals and hygiene, and spend more time at the bedside than almost anyone else on the team. Understanding each link helps you see *why* hand hygiene, PPE, cleaning, and staying home when ill are not optional extras—they are core safety practices.

### Learning objectives

After this lesson you will be able to:

- Name the six links in the chain of infection
- Describe how each link contributes to disease transmission in a nursing facility
- Identify practical CNA actions that break one or more links
- Apply chain-of-infection thinking to everyday care situations

### The six links

Think of the chain as a sequence. Remove or break **any one** link, and infection cannot continue along that pathway.

#### 1. Infectious agent (pathogen)

The infectious agent is the microorganism that can cause disease—bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites. Examples relevant to nursing homes include influenza virus, norovirus, *C. difficile*, MRSA, and the virus that causes COVID-19.

Not every germ causes illness in every situation. The type of organism, how strong it is, and how much of it is present all matter.

#### 2. Reservoir

The reservoir is where the infectious agent lives and multiplies. In long-term care, reservoirs commonly include:

- **People** — residents, staff, or visitors who carry germs with or without symptoms
- **The environment** — contaminated surfaces, shared equipment, soiled linens
- **Water or food** — when handling is unsafe or storage is incorrect

A resident with active diarrhea can be a reservoir for norovirus. A blood-glucose monitor used on multiple residents without proper cleaning can become a reservoir for bloodborne pathogens.

#### 3. Portal of exit

The portal of exit is the path the germ uses to **leave** the reservoir. Examples include:

- Respiratory tract — coughing, sneezing, talking (droplets)
- Gastrointestinal tract — vomit, feces
- Skin or mucous membranes — open wounds, drainage
- Blood — during procedures or accidental needlesticks

A resident who coughs into their hand before touching a call light creates a portal of exit at the respiratory tract and hands.

#### 4. Mode of transmission

This is **how** the germ travels from the reservoir to a new host. Modes you will use in practice include:

- **Contact (direct)** — touch from person to person during care
- **Contact (indirect)** — touch of a contaminated object (bed rail, phone, gait belt)
- **Droplet** — larger respiratory particles that travel short distances (roughly within 3–6 feet), then settle
- **Airborne** — very small particles that can remain suspended and travel on air currents (special precautions apply)
- **Vehicle** — contaminated food, water, medications, or equipment
- **Vector** — living carriers such as insects (less common indoors, but know the term)

In skilled nursing settings, **contact** and **droplet** transmission are among the most common modes you will encounter daily.

#### 5. Portal of entry

The portal of entry is how the germ **gets into** a susceptible host. Common portals include:

- Mucous membranes of the eyes, nose, or mouth
- Non-intact skin — cuts, abrasions, rashes, device sites
- Respiratory tract — inhaling droplets or airborne particles
- Gastrointestinal tract — swallowing contaminated material

Touching your face with contaminated gloves is a classic way staff accidentally create a portal of entry.

#### 6. Susceptible host

A susceptible host is a person who can become infected. In long-term care, many residents have factors that increase susceptibility:

- Advanced age and chronic illness
- Weakened immune response
- Invasive devices (urinary catheters, IV lines, feeding tubes)
- Skin breakdown or wounds
- Medications that affect immunity

Staff can also be susceptible hosts, especially when fatigued, under stress, or working while mildly ill.

### Break one link—stop the spread

Standard infection-control practice is built on a simple principle: **you do not need to fight all six links at once; breaking any single link can stop transmission.**

| CNA practice | Link(s) addressed |
|---|---|
| Hand hygiene before and after resident contact | Mode of transmission, portal of entry |
| Gloves and gown per Standard Precautions / transmission-based precautions | Contact transmission, portal of entry |
| Cleaning and disinfecting high-touch surfaces | Reservoir, indirect contact |
| Proper handling and bagging of soiled linen | Reservoir, mode of transmission |
| Staying home when you have fever, vomiting, or diarrhea | Infectious agent, reservoir |
| Not touching your face while wearing contaminated gloves | Portal of entry |
| Reporting exposures and symptoms promptly | Early intervention across multiple links |

### CNA actions in daily practice

**Hand hygiene** is your most powerful everyday tool. Use facility-approved soap and water when hands are visibly soiled or after caring for residents with known or suspected diarrheal illness; use alcohol-based hand rub when appropriate per policy. Hand hygiene works because it interrupts germs traveling by contact and keeps germs away from your eyes, nose, and mouth.

**Standard Precautions** mean you treat all blood, body fluids, non-intact skin, and mucous membranes as potentially infectious—every resident, every time. Glove use, safe handling of linens, and environmental cleaning are not extra steps for "sick" residents only.

**Transmission-based precautions** (contact, droplet, airborne) add specific PPE and room practices when a resident has a known or suspected infection that spreads by a particular mode. Follow the signage on the door and the care plan. If something is unclear, ask the nurse before entering the room.

**Environmental cleaning** matters because shared surfaces are reservoirs. Wipe high-touch items—bed rails, call buttons, wheelchair handles, over-bed tables—per facility protocol, especially after contact with body fluids.

**Stay home when ill.** Working with a fever, new cough, vomiting, or diarrhea puts residents and coworkers at risk. Follow your facility's return-to-work policy and report illness per occupational health guidelines.

### Case study: Mr. Alvarez, age 78

**Resident profile:** Mr. Alvarez has moderate dementia, uses a walker, and needs assistance with toileting and meals. He shares a semi-private room.

**Scenario:** Over two days, three residents and one CNA on the hall develop vomiting and diarrhea. Mr. Alvarez had mild nausea yesterday but was still sent to the dining room. A CNA helped him to the bathroom, changed his brief with gloves only (no gown), and then served dinner trays on the same hall without re-washing hands. The nurse later suspects a viral gastroenteritis outbreak.

**Discussion prompt:** Which links in the chain of infection were likely involved? What should the CNA have done differently at each step?

### Knowledge checks

**Question 1:** A resident with influenza coughs into the air while you are helping them sit up at the bedside. Which pair best describes the portal of exit and the most likely mode of transmission in this moment?

- Correct answer: Respiratory tract (portal of exit) and droplet transmission (mode of transmission). Influenza spreads when respiratory droplets leave the airway and travel short distances to another person's mucous membranes.

**Question 2:** You finish providing oral care for a resident who has Clostridioides difficile (C. diff) infection. What is the best next step to break the chain?

- Correct answer: Remove gloves, perform hand hygiene with soap and water (per facility policy for C. diff), then don clean gloves before touching the environment or another resident. Alcohol-based hand rub alone does not reliably remove C. diff spores; soap and water helps break contact transmission from the reservoir on your hands.

### Summary

- The chain of infection has six links: infectious agent, reservoir, portal of exit, mode of transmission, portal of entry, and susceptible host.
- All six links must be present for transmission to continue; breaking **any one** link can stop spread.
- CNAs most often break the chain through hand hygiene, Standard Precautions, correct PPE, environmental cleaning, and staying home when ill.
- Apply chain thinking to real situations—outbreaks often involve multiple broken practices, not a single mistake.
- Follow facility policy and notify the nurse when you see patterns that suggest transmission (multiple residents or staff with the same symptoms).
